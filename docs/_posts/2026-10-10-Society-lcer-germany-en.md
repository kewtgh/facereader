In the previous article, we calculated France's Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate (LCER)[^lcer].

The final results were:

> - **Paris: 34.0%.**
> - **Lyon: 27.8%.**

France is one of the most heavily taxed developed countries in the world. Much of the tax and social insurance burden is collected before workers even receive their wages.

So what about Germany?

If France is a high-tax, high-welfare country, Germany cannot be very far behind.

Germany has a fairly comprehensive social security system. Pensions, health insurance, unemployment insurance, long-term care insurance, and various benefits for children and families cover a considerable range of risks in people's lives.

According to the OECD's 2021 figures, the conventional tax wedge[^tax-wedge] for a single, childless German worker earning the national average wage reached **48.1%**—slightly higher than the 47.0% for an equivalent worker in France.

But that is not the LCER we are trying to calculate.

After all, some of the money a German worker pays into the pension system does come back after retirement. Health insurance also pays for medical care when that worker becomes ill. Counting the contributions without counting the benefits would obviously overstate the net cost borne by the family.

So once again, we use the same method as in the previous five countries.

The final results are:

> - **Munich: 38.4%.**
> - **Cologne: 35.3%.**

Both figures are higher than those for Paris and Lyon.

Germany and France also share something else: taxes and social insurance account for the overwhelming majority of LCER in both countries.

But there is something rather unusual about Germany.

Housing in Munich is extremely expensive. In our 2021 model, an ordinary 80-square-meter owner-occupied apartment already costs **€728,000**.

Yet the identifiable institutional housing cost contributes only 0.5% to LCER. Even after adding mortgage interest, the total is just 2.4%.

So I think there are two questions worth exploring in this German article:

**Why does an ordinary German family still bear such a high net institutional cost, even after accounting for the benefits of a fairly comprehensive welfare system?**

And why does Munich's extraordinarily expensive housing contribute so little to its final LCER?

## 1. The same family, this time in Munich and Cologne

The previous five articles have already explained the LCER framework in detail, so I will not start from the beginning again.

We use the same standard family: two working spouses who begin their careers at 25 and raise one child. The real discount rate[^discount-rate] is 2%, with long-run real wage growth assumed to be 0%. They buy a home in their tenth working year, with a 30% down payment and a 70% mortgage.

The normal pension age is set at 67, giving them a 42-year working life.

We still use an 80-square-meter family home. The purchase principal is not counted as extraction because it becomes a household asset. Pension and medical benefits must also be deducted.

This time, Munich is our Leading City[^city-type], and Cologne our Representative City.

Munich has some of the highest wages and productivity among major German cities. It is also one of Germany's most expensive housing markets. Cologne is another major economic center, but its housing costs are considerably lower, making it a more useful reference for an ordinary family in a large German city.

According to the German Federal Employment Agency's 2021 earnings data for employees covered by social insurance[^median-wage], median monthly wages for full-time workers in the two cities were approximately:

- Munich: **€4,681**
- Cologne: **€3,988**

On an annual basis, that means about €56,172 for an ordinary worker in Munich and €47,856 in Cologne.

But as in France, the cost of employing someone is not limited to the wages paid to that person.

German employers must also pay statutory social insurance contributions on top of employees' cash wages. Our model uses an employer contribution rate of approximately 21.1%.

The calculations for the two standard families are therefore:

| Item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Annual wage per worker | €56,172 | €47,856 |
| Annual household cash wages | €112,344 | €95,712 |
| Employer statutory contribution rate | 21.1% | 21.1% |
| Annual household labor value | €135,998 | €115,864 |
| **Lifetime labor value PV**[^pv] | **€3,916,674** | **€3,336,829** |

In other words, a dual-income family in Munich, working from age 25 until retirement at 67, creates a lifetime labor value with a present value of approximately:

**€3.917 million.**

For Cologne, it is approximately:

**€3.337 million.**

These are the denominators for every extraction rate calculated below.

Let me emphasize this point again: the denominator is not the take-home pay received by the family. It is the full labor cost an employer is legally required to pay in order to employ these two people.

Otherwise, the institutional costs deducted before wages reach a worker's account would simply disappear from the calculation.

## 2. German social insurance: the money is allocated before wages are paid

One conspicuous feature of the German social security system is how directly so many payments are tied to labor income.

In 2021, the total contribution rate for the statutory pension system, Gesetzliche Rentenversicherung[^grv], was 18.6%, generally split into 9.3% paid by the employer and 9.3% by the employee.

The general contribution rate for statutory health insurance, Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung (GKV)[^gkv], was 14.6%. Adding the average supplementary contribution of 1.3% in 2021 brings the total to approximately 15.9%.

Then there is unemployment insurance, long-term care insurance, and occupational accident insurance.

Looked at from an ordinary worker's paycheck, the social insurance burden really is substantial.

But this money is not entirely equivalent to tax.

Pension contributions create entitlements to future retirement benefits. Health insurance provides medical services. Unemployment and long-term care insurance protect people against other risks.

That is why we apply the same principle to German pensions and healthcare as we have in the previous countries:

**Mandatory contributions over a lifetime, minus the lifetime benefits attributable to that household.**

Only the difference enters net extraction.

This difference represents the portion of the standard family's contributions that is not returned in the form of corresponding personal benefits under the model. It does not mean that all the money that fails to return to that family's account has been wasted.

Social insurance is designed, in part, for transfers between generations, redistribution across income groups, and the sharing of risk.

But who ultimately receives the money, and how much net cost an ordinary family ends up bearing, are two different questions.

## 3. Pensions: a lot goes in, but how much comes back?

Germany's pension system differs significantly from Britain's.

The UK's State Pension is relatively close to a standardized benefit. Eligibility is linked to contribution records, but the pension does not rise proportionally with a worker's pre-retirement wages.

Germany's statutory pension system, by contrast, is based on pension points, or Entgeltpunkte[^entgeltpunkte].

A person's earnings throughout their career, years of contributions, and earnings relative to the German average all affect their pension after retirement.

Higher earnings generally mean higher contributions and more pension points.

So a family in Munich not only pays more into the pension system during its working years; it can also receive a higher pension after retirement.

How big, then, is the final difference?

In the OECD's long-term pension model, the gross replacement rate[^replacement-rate] for a German worker on average earnings is approximately 41.5%.

But the OECD's full working career begins at age 22, while our series uses 25. So the German model makes an approximate adjustment for 42 years of contributions rather than 45, giving 38.7%.

Nor do we estimate the pension payment period by simply subtracting 67 from life expectancy at birth. We use conditional remaining life expectancy[^conditional-life] for people who have already reached 67.

The results are:

| Pension item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Mandatory pension contributions, PV | €601,794 | €512,701 |
| Pension benefits, PV | €288,126 | €245,470 |
| **Net pension extraction, PV** | **€313,668** | **€267,231** |
| **Share of lifetime labor value** | **8.0%** | **8.0%** |

The Munich family contributes approximately €602,000 in present-value terms over its lifetime and is expected to receive about €288,000 in pension benefits.

The amount that ultimately enters LCER is:

**€314,000, or 8.0%.**

Cologne is also at 8.0%.

Why do the two cities have the same rate?

Because under the current model, both pension contributions and future pension benefits are closely tied to career earnings. Higher income increases contributions, but it also raises retirement benefits.

So although the amounts differ considerably, the net extraction rates as shares of lifetime labor value are almost identical.

Here again we can see a difference between Germany and Britain.

Manchester's wages are significantly lower than London's, but a full UK State Pension does not fall by anything like the same proportion.

In Germany, differences in working-life earnings carry over more directly into pensions.

Still, **an 8.0% net pension cost is roughly one-twelfth of a family's entire lifetime labor value.**

And that is after deducting retirement benefits.

## 4. Healthcare: higher earnings mean higher contributions, not necessarily more care

Germany's statutory health insurance reveals another part of the system.

GKV contributions are largely income-related. Below the contribution ceiling, the higher your wages, the more you pay.

But the amount of medical care a person receives is not directly determined by their income.

The same disease, with the same treatment needs, does not automatically generate 20% more medical benefits just because one person's salary is 20% higher than another's.

In 2021, actual per-capita benefits paid by Germany's statutory health insurance system were approximately €3,594.

Of course, people do not use the same amount of healthcare at every age. Medical spending is generally lower when people are young and rises substantially as they get older.

So the German model adjusts medical benefits by age, estimates healthcare use for both spouses from working age through retirement, and includes the medical benefits received by one child.

This is a model estimate, not a direct observation of every insurance claim made by a real family across its lifetime.

The results are:

| Healthcare item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Health insurance contributions, PV | €537,343 | €457,792 |
| Medical benefits, PV | €275,741 | €275,741 |
| **Net healthcare extraction, PV** | **€261,602** | **€182,051** |
| **Share of lifetime labor value** | **6.7%** | **5.5%** |

The present value of medical benefits is the same for the Munich and Cologne households because the model assumes the same family age structure and patterns of healthcare use.

But the Munich family contributes more.

That gives us:

> - **Munich: 6.7%.**
> - **Cologne: 5.5%.**

This is different from pensions.

Pension contributions and benefits tend to rise together. Higher health insurance contributions, however, do not automatically translate into a proportional increase in medical benefits received by the individual.

Under the same assumptions about age and health, a higher-income household therefore tends to have a larger net health insurance contribution gap.

That is also a reflection of the redistributive character of Germany's social health insurance.

The welfare system we know is not a mechanism that takes everyone's money, holds it in their own account, and returns the exact amount to them in the future.

It links people with different incomes, health conditions, and ages, using contributions from some groups to support the medical needs of others.

This can provide considerable social protection. But on the balance sheet of one particular family over its lifetime, a net cost still exists.

Pensions and healthcare alone already bring net extraction to:

- For a Munich family:

  **8.0% + 6.7% = 14.7%.**

- For Cologne:

  **8.0% + 5.5% = 13.5%.**

And we have not yet counted income tax or consumption taxes.

## 5. German income tax is not low, and spending brings another round of taxes

Germany's personal income tax, Einkommensteuer[^einkommensteuer], is progressive.

As income rises, so does the marginal tax rate.

But that does not mean every euro earned by a higher-income household is taxed at the top marginal rate.

Germany also provides deductions for employment-related expenses and social insurance, joint tax assessment for married couples, child benefits, and tax allowances.

In 2021, Kindergeld[^kindergeld] for the first child was €219 per month.

Our model assumes the child is born in the couple's fifth working year and calculates the corresponding child-related tax and cash benefits over 18 years.

So we are not applying some abstract top tax rate. We are estimating the household's net lifetime income tax burden based on its earnings, deductions, and years of raising a child.

The results:

| Item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Personal income tax, PV | €554,163 | €416,606 |
| **Share of labor value** | **14.2%** | **12.5%** |

Income tax alone takes the equivalent of 14.2% of the Munich family's lifetime labor value.

Cologne is at 12.5%.

And after a family receives its after-tax wages, it still needs to spend money.

Germany's standard VAT[^vat] rate in 2021 was 19%. But not every purchase was subject to that rate, and not all labor income was spent in the year it was earned.

So we have not simply multiplied all household consumption by 19%.

Using German household spending patterns and research on taxes at the household level, the model estimates the VAT burden actually borne by families in different income groups, along with energy, vehicle, and other consumption taxes.

The results:

| Consumption tax item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| VAT, PV | €207,069 | €187,439 |
| VAT as share of labor value | 5.3% | 5.6% |
| Other consumption taxes, PV | €64,709 | €60,642 |
| Other consumption taxes as share of labor value | 1.7% | 1.8% |

Consumption taxes total about 7.0% for Munich and 7.4% for Cologne.

Cologne pays less in absolute terms, but slightly more as a proportion of lifetime labor value.

This reflects a familiar feature of indirect taxation: lower-income households often need to spend a greater share of their disposable income on everyday consumption. As a result, consumption taxes as a share of labor value do not necessarily fall when income falls.

Now we can put pensions, healthcare, income tax, and consumption taxes together.

| Fiscal and social insurance item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net pension extraction | 8.0% | 8.0% |
| Net healthcare extraction | 6.7% | 5.5% |
| Personal income tax | 14.2% | 12.5% |
| VAT | 5.3% | 5.6% |
| Other consumption taxes | 1.7% | 1.8% |
| **Net fiscal/social insurance extraction** | **35.8%** | **33.4%** |

*Individual percentages have been rounded; totals use the unrounded amounts in the model. For unemployment, long-term care, and occupational accident insurance, the present model conservatively treats expected lifetime benefits as equal to contributions, giving a net extraction of zero. This does not mean no contributions are paid.*

This is the largest component of Germany's LCER.

**35.8% for Munich, 33.4% for Cologne.**



And those figures are already net of pension and healthcare benefits.

They are not the result of simply adding together tax rates. They are the net fiscal and social insurance costs borne by the standard family over its lifetime, after deducting the identifiable benefits returned to it.

When we calculated France, this component came to 31.2% for Paris and 25.6% for Lyon.

Both German cities are higher.

Of course, pensions, healthcare financing, and wage distributions differ across countries. These four figures alone do not establish that every group in Germany faces a heavier tax burden than its counterpart in France.

But for the two types of ordinary dual-income household modeled here, Germany's net fiscal and social insurance burden is substantial.

## 6. Housing in Munich is expensive, but Munich is not Shanghai

Now let's look at housing.

This may be the most counterintuitive part of the German calculation.

In 2021, the representative transaction price for an existing owner-occupied apartment in Munich was around €9,100 per square meter.

Using our standard 80-square-meter home:

**€728,000.**

For Cologne, the model uses a representative price of approximately €4,620 per square meter:

**€369,600.**

That gives us:

| Housing item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Price of an 80 m² home | €728,000 | €369,600 |
| Home price / annual household wages | **6.48×** | **3.86×** |

A home in Munich costs almost twice as much as one in Cologne.

And a price-to-income ratio of 6.48 is already quite high for an ordinary dual-income family.

So why, if Munich's housing is so expensive, is the institutional extraction associated with housing so low?

This brings us back to a point I discussed repeatedly in the China article:

**High housing prices do not necessarily mean an equivalent amount of institutional extraction.**

When we calculated Shanghai's LCER at 101.8%, the most important factor was not the construction cost of an ordinary apartment. It was the institutional cost embedded in land finance, followed by the housing leverage and long-term financing costs generated by high property prices.

Germany does not have that kind of land-finance structure.

Germany certainly has land-supply restrictions, urban planning rules, building regulations, and other institutions that affect housing supply. These can push up housing prices.

For example, in 2021 the Bundesbank estimated that housing prices in some German cities were overvalued by approximately 10%–30% relative to fundamentals.

But that kind of overvaluation cannot automatically be counted as institutional extraction.

Housing prices can be affected by land scarcity, rising incomes, population concentration, low interest rates, expectations of future appreciation, and planning restrictions, among other things.

Without sufficiently reliable counterfactual research separating normal market prices from the additional costs caused by institutional restrictions, we cannot simply put the entire housing premium into LCER.

So for now, the German model includes only the real estate transfer tax that can be clearly identified:

**Grunderwerbsteuer[^grunderwerbsteuer].**

In 2021:

- Bavaria, where Munich is located: **3.5%**
- North Rhine-Westphalia, where Cologne is located: **6.5%**

In other words, housing in Munich costs almost twice as much as in Cologne, but the real estate transfer tax rate is actually lower.

Assuming the home is purchased in the tenth working year and discounting the cost back to age 25:

| Institutional housing cost | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Real estate transfer tax rate | 3.5% | 6.5% |
| Institutional housing cost, PV | €20,902 | €19,708 |
| **Share of lifetime labor value** | **0.5%** | **0.6%** |

**Just 0.5% and 0.6%.**

This does not mean that the German government collects no taxes from real estate.

Nor does it mean Germany's housing market has no institutional problems.

Annual property tax, Grundsteuer[^grundsteuer], the supply constraints created by planning, building regulatory costs, and other charges that have not yet been reliably quantified are not fully included in this central estimate.

But one thing is clear: Germany's housing system cannot simply be analyzed using Shanghai's land-finance model.

The fact that housing in Munich is expensive is not, on its own, evidence that the government extracts the same proportion of household wealth through real estate.

## 7. German mortgage rates were low, so financing costs were low as well

High housing prices do not necessarily mean mortgage costs rise by the same proportion.

Germany was in a prolonged low-interest-rate environment in 2021.

Long-term fixed mortgage rates reported by the Bundesbank were already very low. The rate used in our model is approximately:

**1.3%.**

We keep the same down payment and loan structure as in the previous countries:

- 30% down payment
- 70% mortgage
- 30-year loan term
- Fully amortizing[^amortization]

For the sake of a consistent static comparison based on 2021 conditions, the model also assumes that any subsequent refinancing takes place at the same interest rate.

The results are:

| Mortgage item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Home price | €728,000 | €369,600 |
| Mortgage principal | €509,600 | €258,720 |
| Mortgage interest rate | 1.3% | 1.3% |
| Mortgage interest, PV | €72,332 | €36,722 |
| **Share of lifetime labor value** | **1.9%** | **1.1%** |

To buy the €728,000 apartment, the Munich family needs to borrow €509,600.

Yet the interest on that 30-year loan, discounted back to the beginning of the family's working life, comes to only about €72,300.

That is 1.9% of its lifetime labor value.

In Cologne, the figure is just 1.1%.

This is a very different result from the China article.

Mortgage interest alone amounted to 30.8% in Shanghai, compared with just 1.9% in Munich.

Of course, this does not mean that everyone who bought a home in Germany in 2021 could lock in a 1.3% rate for the next 30 years.

The model uses a static 2021 interest rate. If refinancing rates rise in the future, mortgage costs will rise too.

But using the same base year, Germany's housing-finance structure is clearly very different from Shanghai's.

**Housing in Munich is expensive, but in this model those high prices do not translate into similarly enormous mortgage-interest costs.**

That also explains why mortgage financing has not become a major source of Germany's LCER, despite Munich's price-to-income ratio of 6.48.

## 8. Germany has another unusual mandatory charge: Rundfunkbeitrag

Germany has another rather interesting institution:

**Rundfunkbeitrag[^rundfunkbeitrag], the public broadcasting contribution.**

An ordinary household that meets the statutory criteria generally has to pay it. Watching very little public television does not, by itself, exempt a family from the charge.

From January through July 2021, the contribution was €17.50 per household per month.

In August, it rose to €18.36.

That gives an annual standard contribution of approximately:

**€214.30.**

This does not look like a large amount. But if a family pays it from early adulthood through retirement, and we extend the calculation to the longer life expectancy of the two spouses, the cumulative amount is not entirely negligible.

In present-value terms, it comes to approximately **€7,689**.

That represents:

- Munich: **0.20%**
- Cologne: **0.23%**

I have included it in Extended LCER[^extended-lcer], rather than Core LCER.

The reason is straightforward.

Public broadcasting does provide services, and it may generate public value. But a mandatory charge assessed per dwelling does not correspond to a clearly identifiable household asset or an individual cash benefit that the family can claim.

So it makes more sense to report it separately as a Germany-specific mandatory institutional expense.

In the overall German LCER, however, it remains a very small component.

There are also other costs in Germany that have not been fully included.

Education, for example.

Education spending accounts for approximately 0.7% of ordinary German household consumption, below the 1.5% Japanese benchmark for education competition used consistently in this series[^education-baseline]. So under the current model, there is no identifiable excess education-competition cost.

This does not mean education in Germany is costless, or that public education requires no government spending.

It simply means that, under the model we established earlier, German households do not incur an additional excess education-competition expense that qualifies for inclusion.

For unemployment insurance, long-term care insurance, and occupational accident insurance, sufficiently reliable lifetime benefit probabilities for a standard household are not available. We therefore provisionally treat contributions and benefits as equal, with net extraction set at zero.

Housing scarcity premiums created by planning restrictions, annual property taxes, and some costs arising from energy and public utility regulation are also excluded from the central estimate for now.

These omitted costs are not necessarily small.

We simply do not yet have sufficiently reliable data to express them as net lifetime costs for the standard household without double-counting items already included elsewhere.

## 9. Putting Germany's numbers together

Now let's put all the items that can be clearly quantified into one account.

### Munich

Net fiscal and social insurance extraction: **35.8%.**

Institutional housing cost: **0.5%.**

Mortgage interest: **1.9%.**

This gives a **Core LCER of 38.2%.**

Then we add the public broadcasting contribution and other identified Germany-specific mandatory costs: **0.2%.**

The final result:

> **Total LCER: 38.4%.**

Against a lifetime household labor value of approximately €3.917 million, the institutional net costs included in the model come to roughly:

**€1.502 million.**

### Cologne

Net fiscal and social insurance extraction: **33.4%.**

Institutional housing cost: **0.6%.**

Mortgage interest: **1.1%.**

This gives a **Core LCER of 35.1%.**

Then we add Germany-specific mandatory costs: **0.2%.**

The final result:

> **Total LCER: 35.3%.**

Against a lifetime household labor value of approximately €3.337 million, the institutional net costs included in the model come to roughly:

**€1.178 million.**

The full results are:

| Item | Munich | Cologne |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net fiscal/social insurance extraction | **35.8%** | **33.4%** |
| Institutional housing cost | 0.5% | 0.6% |
| Mortgage interest | 1.9% | 1.1% |
| Excess education competition | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| Premium due to housing supply restrictions | Not Included[^not-included] | Not Included |
| Other institutional costs not yet quantified | Not Included | Not Included |
| **Core LCER** | **38.2%** | **35.1%** |
| Public broadcasting contribution and other extended items | 0.2% | 0.2% |
| **Total / Extended LCER** | **38.4%** | **35.3%** |

The gap between the two cities is 3.1 percentage points.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-germany-the-government-took-the-money-02.jpg" alt="ai-germany-the-government-took-the-money-02" /></figure>

What is interesting is that this difference is not mainly driven by institutional housing taxes.

Munich's income tax burden is higher. Its family also contributes more to health insurance, without receiving medical benefits that rise proportionally with income. These differences push Munich's net extraction rate upward.

Housing finance contributes to the gap as well, but accounts for only about 0.8 percentage points.

So this is not a housing-cost divergence resembling Shanghai versus Nanjing.

Germany's main institutional costs are much more directly tied to labor income.

## 10. We now have six countries

With Germany added, we have completed six countries and twelve cities.

| Country | City | Category | LCER |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| China | Shanghai | Leading City | **101.8%** |
| China | Nanjing | Representative City | **75.3%** |
| United States | New York | Leading City | **30.3%** |
| United States | Chicago | Representative City | **23.0%** |
| Canada | Toronto | Leading City | **34.1%** |
| Canada | Winnipeg | Representative City | **26.7%** |
| United Kingdom | London | Leading City | **27.7%** |
| United Kingdom | Manchester | Representative City | **17.0%** |
| France | Paris | Leading City | **34.0%** |
| France | Lyon | Representative City | **27.8%** |
| Germany | Munich | Leading City | **38.4%** |
| Germany | Cologne | Representative City | **35.3%** |

*Germany is reported using Extended LCER; the other countries use the final figures published in their earlier articles. Some institutional costs have not yet been monetized in all countries. These figures should therefore be understood as central estimates under a common framework, not as an international welfare ranking accurate to one decimal place.*

Some differences are becoming quite clear.

Take Toronto at 34.1% and Paris at 34.0%. The numbers are almost identical.

But in Canada, Toronto's net fiscal and social insurance extraction is only 19.6%. A substantial part of the remainder comes from institutional housing costs and financing.

For Paris, by contrast, 31.2% comes from net fiscal and social insurance extraction.

Munich takes this even further:

**At 35.8%, Munich's net fiscal and social insurance extraction alone exceeds the entire LCER of both Toronto and Paris.**

Meanwhile, institutional housing costs and mortgage interest in Munich add up to just 2.4%.

Cologne is interesting too.

Its 35.3% is not only higher than Lyon's 27.8%; it is also higher than Paris's 34.0%.

In other words, Germany does not have to rely on its most expensive city, Munich, to produce a high Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate.

Even in Cologne, a large and relatively representative city where an ordinary dual-income family does not face Munich's housing prices, more than one-third of lifetime labor value is absorbed by the institutional net costs identified in the current model.

But we cannot simply take these figures to mean that Germany has the “worst welfare system.”

What LCER currently compares is the institutional cash flows and attributable benefits that can be identified, estimated, and netted under a common set of rules. It does not fully measure the value that public safety, basic education, infrastructure, and other public services provide to residents.

So we can compare the identifiable net costs borne by families. LCER alone cannot tell us which country has the best overall quality of life or the most efficient public spending.

And because German planning restrictions, annual property taxes, and other items have not been fully included, these estimates may still change as more research becomes available.

At the very least, the German results have made one thing clear.

## 11. In Germany, the money is taken during the process of earning it

When I wrote about France, I made an observation:

The French government is very good at plucking the goose. A great deal of money is collected through taxes and social insurance before it ever reaches the worker's account.

Looking at Germany now, much the same mechanism is evident.

Germany offers ordinary working families comprehensive pensions, healthcare, and other social protections. But it also requires workers and employers to make substantial contributions year after year.

Some of that money eventually returns to households in the form of pensions and healthcare. The rest supports redistribution within the social security system and other public expenditure.

Looking at the lifetime accounts of one ordinary family, even after deducting the benefits we can identify, Germany still leaves it with a substantial net institutional cost.

Unlike Shanghai, the German government does not need large land-sale revenues and enormous mortgage-interest payments to impose substantial institutional costs on households.

**In Germany, extraction happens primarily while labor value is being created, wages are being distributed, and households are spending their income.**

Munich: 38.4%. Cologne: 35.3%.

These two numbers tell us something:

**Even a country with a comprehensive public welfare system does not necessarily leave ordinary families with a larger share of the labor value they create.**

Whether welfare benefits can offset those costs cannot be answered by attaching the label “high-welfare country.”

We have to account for the money paid in and the benefits that come back, item by item.

[^lcer]: **LCER (Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate)** is the measure used in this series: the present value of identifiable net institutional costs over a household's lifetime, divided by the present value of its lifetime labor value. It covers taxes and mandatory social insurance, as well as housing institutions, financing, and other institutional costs that meet the model's criteria. Pension and healthcare benefits attributable to the household are deducted. LCER is not a conventional tax rate, nor does it measure the quality of public services on its own.

[^tax-wedge]: **Tax wedge** is the difference between the employer's total labor cost and a worker's net earnings after personal income tax, employee social insurance contributions, and related deductions, expressed as a share of total employer labor cost. The figures of 48.1% for Germany and 47.0% for France cited here are OECD statistics for **single, childless workers earning the average wage** in 2021. They are not directly comparable to the lifetime net costs of the dual-income, one-child family modeled in this article. See [OECD, *Taxing Wages 2022*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/taxing-wages-2022_f7f1e68a-en.html).

[^discount-rate]: **Real discount rate** is the rate used to convert future wages, contributions, benefits, and other costs into values at one common point in time, after removing the effect of inflation. This series assumes a 2% real discount rate and 0% long-run real wage growth. Both are modeling assumptions, not forecasts of Germany's economic future.

[^city-type]: **Leading City / Representative City** refers to the two-city comparison used for each country in this series. The leading city generally has higher wages, productivity, or housing costs; the representative city offers a view of a more ordinary large-city household. These two cities cannot represent every region or income group in a country.

[^median-wage]: **Median earnings of employees covered by social insurance** are calculated from registered employment and earnings data in Germany's social insurance system. The median is the point above which half of the observations fall and below which the other half fall. The figures here cover full-time employees subject to social insurance, not necessarily part-time workers, the self-employed, civil servants, or the entire population. See the [compilation of regional German median earnings for 2021](https://biaj.de/images/2022-07-25_entgelt-median-laender-und-kreisvergleich-wohnort-arbeitsort-2021.pdf).

[^pv]: **PV (Present Value)** is the value of income or expenditure from different years after discounting it to a common point in time. In this article, all amounts are discounted back to the age of 25, when the couple begins working. “Lifetime labor value PV” is therefore not the simple sum of undiscounted wages earned throughout the household's life.

[^grv]: **Gesetzliche Rentenversicherung (German statutory pension insurance)** is a central part of Germany's public retirement income system. Contributions from current workers largely finance pensions paid to existing retirees. Individual benefits depend on contribution histories, pension points, and statutory calculation rules. The standard total contribution rate in 2021 was 18.6%, generally split equally between employer and employee. See [Deutsche Rentenversicherung](https://www.deutsche-rentenversicherung.de/).

[^gkv]: **Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung (GKV, German statutory health insurance)** is Germany's main statutory sickness insurance system. Contributions are based on earnings, while benefits are provided according to medical need. In 2021, the general contribution rate was 14.6%, with an additional fund-specific contribution. The 1.3% cited here is the average supplementary rate for that year. Contributions are subject to a statutory income ceiling.

[^entgeltpunkte]: **Entgeltpunkte (pension points)** are one of the fundamental measures used to calculate an individual's German statutory pension. Relevant earnings in each contribution year are compared with average earnings for that year, generating pension points. Retirement benefits depend on accumulated points and other statutory factors. This is not an arrangement in which a person's contributions are simply deposited unchanged into an individual investment account.

[^replacement-rate]: **Gross replacement rate** is the ratio of gross pension income to earnings before retirement, prior to deductions such as income tax. The 41.5% figure in the article comes from the OECD pension model. The 38.7% figure is an approximate adjustment for this series' assumption that work begins at 25 and continues for 42 years; it is not a single pension replacement rate prescribed for all German retirees. See [OECD, *Pensions at a Glance 2021*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2021_ca401ebd-en.html).

[^conditional-life]: **Conditional remaining life expectancy** is the expected number of further years of life given that a person has already reached a specified age. To estimate a pension beginning at 67, we should use the remaining life expectancy of people who have reached 67, not “life expectancy at birth minus 67.”

[^einkommensteuer]: **Einkommensteuer (German personal income tax)** is a progressive tax on an individual's taxable income. A marginal tax rate applies only to income within the relevant bracket; it is not the average effective tax rate on all earnings. Joint assessment for married couples, deductible social insurance contributions, and child-related tax provisions can all affect the final burden. See the [German Federal Ministry of Finance, Income Tax Act §32a (2021)](https://amtliche-handbuecher.bundesfinanzministerium.de/esth/2021/A-Einkommensteuergesetz/IV-Tarif/Paragraf-32a/inhalt.html).

[^kindergeld]: **Kindergeld (child benefit)** is a cash benefit paid in Germany to eligible families. In 2021, the monthly amount was €219 for each of the first two children. Germany also has a child tax allowance, **Kinderfreibetrag**. The tax authorities compare the relevant forms of relief under the applicable rules; they cannot simply be added together without adjustment.

[^vat]: **VAT (Value Added Tax)** is known in Germany as *Umsatzsteuer* or *Mehrwertsteuer*. The standard rate in 2021 was 19%, while some goods and services qualified for a reduced 7% rate or an exemption. A household's actual VAT burden depends on how much it spends and what it buys. The 19% rate therefore cannot simply be applied to its entire wage income.

[^grunderwerbsteuer]: **Grunderwerbsteuer (real estate transfer tax)** is levied on qualifying real estate transfers in Germany, with rates determined by the federal states. In 2021 the rate was 3.5% in Bavaria and 6.5% in North Rhine-Westphalia. It is a transaction tax paid in connection with property acquisition, distinct from the annual property tax, Grundsteuer.

[^grundsteuer]: **Grundsteuer (annual property tax)** is a recurring local tax on land and real estate, distinct from Grunderwerbsteuer, which is paid when property is acquired. The amount depends on the property's assessed tax base, the applicable tax calculation factors, and local rates. This article does not include it in the central estimate because sufficiently reliable data for the two standardized properties were not available.

[^amortization]: **Fully amortizing mortgage** means a loan repaid through regular payments of principal and interest, leaving no outstanding principal balance at the end of its term. In this LCER model, only financing interest is counted as extraction. Principal repayment is excluded because repaying principal also reduces the household's debt.

[^rundfunkbeitrag]: **Rundfunkbeitrag (German public broadcasting contribution)** is a statutory charge generally assessed per dwelling rather than per device to support public broadcasting. Statutory exemptions and reductions exist. Whether a household actually watches or listens to public broadcasting generally does not determine its obligation to pay. The monthly amount was €17.50 from January through July 2021 and €18.36 beginning in August. See the [official public broadcasting contribution explanation](https://www.rundfunkbeitrag.de/presse-und-aktuelles/hinweise/beitragsanpassung-2021/haeufige-fragen-zur-beitragsanpassung).

[^extended-lcer]: **Core LCER / Extended LCER** distinguishes the core net institutional costs that can be quantified under the cross-country framework from an extended estimate that adds clearly identified and monetizable country-specific mandatory costs. In Germany, Rundfunkbeitrag is included in Extended LCER, not Core LCER. Whether extended items are included must be considered when comparing countries.

[^education-baseline]: **Excess education-competition benchmark** is a model threshold used in this series: education spending equivalent to 1.5% of household consumption, with Japan serving as a reference. Only spending above this threshold that also satisfies the model's other conditions is included as excess education competition. This is a modeling convention, not a claim that Germany has no education costs or educational inequality.

[^not-included]: **Not Included** means a potential institutional cost has been identified but has not been added to this LCER estimate because reliable, applicable household-level amounts are unavailable or double-counting cannot yet be ruled out. It is different from a measured value of 0%, and it does not mean that the underlying cost does not exist.
