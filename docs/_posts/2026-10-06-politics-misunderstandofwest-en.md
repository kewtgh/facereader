When we talk about modern world history, it is very easy to form a certain impression:

**Western civilization is a civilization unusually fond of aggression.**

- The Age of Exploration.
- Colonialism.
- The slave trade.
- The Spanish conquest of the Americas.
- British rule in India.
- The Opium Wars.
- The partition of Africa.
- And then, later, two world wars.

If we compressed the last five hundred years of world history into a set of images, warships, cannons, colonies, white armies, and conquered peoples would certainly occupy a very prominent place.

So a conclusion that sounds perfectly natural appears:

> The West conquered the world because Western civilization is more aggressive than other civilizations.

I have seen versions of this argument many times.

But there is a very simple question here that people rarely keep asking:

> 1. **Before Europeans arrived in India, what were Indians doing?**
> 2. **Before the Spanish landed in the Americas, what was the relationship among the political powers already there?**
> 3. **Before European colonialism appeared, were the Islamic world, China, and Europe itself living in peace?**

Stretch the historical timeline just a little further back, and the answer becomes rather uncomfortable:

**Aggression was never invented by Western civilization.**

It is difficult even to describe it as a special property of any one civilization.

It looks much more like a recurring condition of human political society.

------

## India Was Not Living in Peace Before the British Conquered It

Let us start with India.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-indian-civilization.jpg" alt="ai-indian-civilization" /></figure>

When colonialism is discussed today, it is easy to begin with the Battle of Plassey in 1757 and understand the story like this:

The British arrived, and then an ancient civilization called India was conquered.

There is nothing wrong with the second half of that sentence.

Britain did conquer India.

But if we go one step further and imagine that, before the British arrived, the Indian subcontinent was a world in which different political powers respected one another and coexisted peacefully, then we are no longer talking about history.

As early as the third century BCE, Ashoka of the Mauryan Empire launched a war of conquest against Kalinga.

Interestingly, we do not even need later British historians to tell us about the war. Ashoka left a record himself.

In his Thirteenth Rock Edict, he wrote that after Kalinga was conquered, 150,000 people were deported, 100,000 were killed, and many more died from other consequences of the war. It was precisely this destruction that later led Ashoka to feel deep remorse over warfare.

These numbers, of course, should not be treated as precise casualty statistics in the modern sense. But one thing is very clear:

**One of the most famous rulers in Indian history was himself a conqueror before he became the Ashoka we later remember for his devotion to Dharma.**

Move forward to the Mughal period, and the basic pattern does not fundamentally change.

Akbar is rightly praised today for religious tolerance, cultural integration, and administrative ability.

But Akbar was also a remarkably successful imperial expansionist.

In 1568 he captured Chittor; Ranthambore followed; Gujarat was annexed in 1572–1573; later Kashmir, Sindh, and parts of the Deccan also came under Mughal control. Relevant materials from the Metropolitan Museum of Art and *Encyclopaedia Iranica* describe these regions quite plainly as territories gradually incorporated into the empire through military expansion.

Under Aurangzeb, Mughal expansion pushed further into the Deccan and became entangled in prolonged war with the Marathas.

So if we erased the British entirely from the map, would Indian history suddenly become a history of peace?

Of course not.

Kingdoms would still swallow kingdoms.

Empires would still expand.

Rulers would still go to war over land, taxes, ports, trade routes, and security.

This is not a defense of British colonialism in India.

The damage caused by British rule, and the question of whether Britain was the first power ever to conquer other people in India, are two completely different questions.

What I am trying to say is much simpler:

**India did not first encounter “conquest” when the British arrived.**

## The Islamic World Was Never a Single Peaceful and Unified Whole

Now look at Islamic civilization.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-islamic-civilization.jpg" alt="ai-islamic-civilization" /></figure>

We often use the phrase “the Islamic world” today. The phrase can easily create an illusion that historically it was a highly unified civilizational community.

It was not.

One of the deepest political traumas in early Islamic history was a civil war among Muslims themselves.

In 656, after the third caliph, Uthman, was killed, disputes over succession, revenge, and political authority quickly developed into the First ***Fitna***, the first Islamic civil war. The Battle of the Camel followed in 656, and Ali emerged victorious.

But the war did not end there. Conflict between Mu'awiya and Ali continued, culminating in the Battle of Siffin in 657.

Ali was eventually assassinated, and the Umayyad dynasty was established.

This was not a war between “Islam” and some other civilization. It was a war among Muslims—and the story did not end there.

In 680, Husayn, the grandson of the Prophet Muhammad, was killed by Umayyad forces at Karbala.

The battle itself was not especially large in military terms, but it later became one of the most important events in Shi'a collective memory and profoundly shaped the historical division between Sunni and Shi'a Islam.

Move forward several centuries.

After the sixteenth century, two very powerful Muslim empires dominated much of the Middle East.

To the west stood the Sunni Ottoman Empire.

To the east stood the Safavid Empire, which made Shi'a Islam the state religion.

If belonging to the same religious civilization naturally made political communities more peaceful toward one another, then perhaps these two empires should have stopped fighting because both were Muslim.

History says the opposite.

The Battle of Chaldiran in 1514 was only the beginning.

For more than a century, the two empires repeatedly fought over eastern Anatolia, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, and Baghdad.

Sectarian conflict was indeed an important part of this rivalry, and both the Ottomans and Safavids used religious language to mobilize support and justify their legitimacy. But over time, geopolitics, borders, trade, and imperial expansion became increasingly important as well.

The Peace of Amasya in 1555 did not end the rivalry. More wars followed, and it was not until the Treaty of Zuhab in 1639 that the frontier became relatively stable.

So it is difficult to say that war came from “Western civilization.”

There is no West in this story at all.

Both sides were Muslim empires.

They fought over land, borders, resources, religious legitimacy, control of Baghdad, and the question of who would be the dominant regional power.

That is much closer to the normal pattern of human history.

## The Americas Were Not an Eden Before Europeans Arrived

The Americas are even more interesting.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-american-civilization.jpg" alt="ai-american-civilization" /></figure>

We are very familiar with the standard images of European colonization in the Americas:

The Spanish arrived, and the Aztec Empire collapsed.

Disease, war, and colonial rule produced catastrophic consequences for Indigenous peoples.

But if we stop the story there, another image easily takes shape:

Before Europeans arrived, Indigenous peoples lived in harmony with nature and in relative peace with one another.

Then Europeans brought war.

The problem is that the Aztec Empire itself was built through military expansion.

After the Mexica formed the Triple Alliance with Texcoco and Tlacopan in the fifteenth century, they repeatedly used military force to bring surrounding regions under control and built an extensive tribute network.

War produced not only territory, but tribute, political submission, and captives. Smithsonian materials on Aztec political organization quite literally use terms such as “new conquests,” “tribute networks,” and “imperial expansion.”

Tlaxcala is even more revealing.

It was never fully conquered by the Aztecs, and the two sides fought repeatedly, including the famous Flower Wars.

So when Hernán Cortés arrived in 1519, what happened was not that a few hundred Spaniards single-handedly defeated a vast American empire.

What actually happened was that Cortés found extremely important Indigenous allies. The most important among them were the Tlaxcalans, long-standing enemies of the Aztecs.

The Smithsonian's own summary of the conquest of Mexico is very direct: without Tlaxcalan and other anti-Aztec Indigenous warriors, Cortés's small Spanish force could not have completed the siege and destruction of Tenochtitlan.

Think about what that means.

**The Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire itself made use of wars and political hatreds that already existed among Indigenous peoples of the Americas.**

This does not reduce the responsibility of Spanish colonizers for what followed.

But it tells us something important:

Europeans did not enter a politically empty continent.

There were already empires.

There were already subjects.

There was tribute.

There was war.

There were old enemies.

There were alliances.

And there were people who wanted to use a newly arrived external force to settle conflicts with old rivals.

---

North America was no different.

In the seventeenth century, the Haudenosaunee, commonly known as the Iroquois Confederacy, engaged in prolonged military expansion during the so-called Beaver Wars and fought a number of surrounding Indigenous political communities.

Of course, this was already after European arrival. The fur trade and European firearms clearly changed the scale of warfare and the balance of power, so it cannot simply be treated as a purely “pre-Columbian” case.

But it reminds us of something else:

**Indigenous peoples of the Americas were not passive characters on the historical stage who only existed to be invaded by others.**

Like people everywhere else, they formed alliances, competed, expanded, retaliated, adopted new weapons, and tried to control resources and trade.

Smithsonian research on Indigenous migration and warfare in North America likewise notes that warfare was hardly unknown in North American history and prehistory before European contact.

**To describe Indigenous peoples as permanently peaceful until Europeans arrived and taught them war may look sympathetic.**

**In reality, it still turns them from human beings into an imagined symbol.**

------

## China Was No Different

Chinese readers should probably find this the easiest case to understand.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-chinese-civilization.jpg" alt="ai-chinese-civilization" /></figure>

We simply do not usually use words like “colonialism” or “imperialism” to describe our own ancient history.

We prefer phrases such as:

> - unification
> - territorial expansion
> - dynastic transition
> - pacifying rebellion
> - contending for the realm

But strip those words away for a moment.

What is underneath?

- more than a century of warfare among the Warring States, ending with Qin's conquest of the six rival states
- the Chu-Han Contention
- the Three Kingdoms
- the Northern and Southern Dynasties
- the wars at the end of the Sui
- the An Lushan Rebellion
- the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms
- the wars among the Song, Liao, Jin, and Yuan
- the Ming-Qing transition

Would the ordinary people living through those wars feel that what they experienced was somehow not conquest simply because later historians wrote the words “all under Heaven was unified”?

The political unifications of Chinese history were themselves the outcome of countless wars.

And every major dynastic transition in Chinese history caused abnormal population losses of at least one-third, and in many cases the figure was as high as one-half.

Because we now share a common historical narrative, we tend to treat Qin's conquest of Chu, the Yuan conquest of the Song, and the Qing conquest of the Ming as “China's own history.”

But for the people living through those wars, a besieged city was still a besieged city, and a destroyed home was still a destroyed home.

A person who was killed did not die any more gently simply because, two thousand years later, both sides would be written into the same book called *A History of China*.

------

## So I Do Not Really Believe the Explanation That “Westerners Simply Like Aggression More”

At this point, we can ask the original question again.

If conquest existed in India,

if it existed in China,

if it existed in the Islamic world,

and if it existed in the Americas,

then Europe hardly needs special treatment. The Roman Empire, the Hundred Years' War, the Thirty Years' War, dynastic wars, religious wars—Europeans spent an enormous amount of history killing one another.

So why, in the modern era, did aggression become so closely associated with “the West”?

I do not think the real answer is:

Europeans suddenly developed a desire for conquest that no other people possessed.

The real answer is:

**Europeans acquired a capacity for conquest that no other civilization had previously possessed.**

Those two sentences sound similar.

They are not.

------

## What Was Truly Distinctive About the West Was That It Pushed an Ancient Human Capacity for Conquest to a Different Order of Magnitude

Earlier empires could also be extraordinarily powerful:

- Rome could dominate the Mediterranean.
- The Mongols could cross Eurasia.
- The Ottoman Empire could rule large territories in the Balkans, the Middle East, and North Africa.
- Chinese empires also governed vast territories for long periods of time.

But all of them faced one obvious constraint:

**Distance.**

In the ancient world, distance limited the reach of empire because:

Armies had to physically get there.

Food had to be transported there.

Orders had to reach there.

Taxes had to come back.

The farther an empire expanded, the more expensive it became to maintain control.

Most empires therefore had a natural radius of effective power.

What modern Europe changed was precisely that limit.

- ocean-going navigation
- artillery
- mapping and surveying
- joint-stock companies
- insurance
- banks
- state credit and public debt
- professional armies
- industrial production
- railways
- steamships
- the telegraph
- and later, increasingly sophisticated modern bureaucratic systems

None of these things was invented by a single person on a single day.

But once they were combined, something unprecedented—and frightening—happened:

**For the first time, a state could project its power reliably to the other side of the planet.**

Britain could make policy in London and collect taxes in India.

It could fight wars along the coast of China.

It could run plantations in the Caribbean.

It could establish colonial governments in Africa.

It could send settlers continuously to Australia.

And all of these places could be connected through shipping, finance, military force, and administration.

Europe did not invent war.

It did not invent empire.

It did not invent slavery.

What it achieved was something else:

**It globalized conquest.**

That is what was truly distinctive about modern Western colonialism.

It was not that European desires were fundamentally different from everyone else's.

It was that those desires, for the first time, acquired a global execution system.

------

## We Easily Mistake a Difference in Capability for a Difference in Moral Nature

This is, in my view, one of the biggest mistakes in how Western civilization is discussed today.

Imagine that the Aztec Empire of the sixteenth century suddenly possessed the Royal Navy, the steam engine, a modern financial system, and an industrial arms industry.

Would it have stayed quietly inside the Valley of Mexico?

I cannot say with certainty that it would have expanded across the globe. History does not allow that kind of proof.

But if we look at what the Aztecs already did within the limits of their actual capabilities, it is difficult to declare with confidence:

They definitely would not have expanded.

The same applies elsewhere.

If the Mughal Empire had been able to cross the Indian Ocean at low cost and continuously send hundreds of thousands of soldiers and administrators to another continent, why should we assume that some uniquely Indian civilizational morality would have stopped it?

The Ottoman Empire already expanded into as much of Europe, Asia, and Africa as it could effectively control.

Did it stop because it suddenly discovered that imperialism was morally wrong?

Or because the cost of further expansion kept rising?

The answer is not especially difficult.

So I am inclined toward a very simple judgment:

**Many civilizations did not conquer the world not necessarily because they loved peace more.**

**They may simply have lacked the capacity to conquer the world.**

That is not an excuse for the West.

Quite the opposite.

Once the West acquired that capacity, it used it.

And it used it thoroughly.

------

## The Damage Caused by Western Colonialism Was Real

There is no need to evade this point.

Saying that other civilizations also invaded and conquered does not mean:

So European colonialism was no big deal.

That is a completely different argument.

Western colonial expansion brought the slave trade, land seizure, mass death, resource extraction, cultural destruction, political domination, and, in many places, institutionalized racial hierarchies.

Those are historical facts.

And precisely because the West possessed organizational and technological capacities far beyond those of earlier empires, its capacity to inflict damage was magnified as well.

An ancient kingdom might win a war and occupy a few neighboring cities.

An industrial empire could redraw the political boundaries of an entire continent, alter the tax systems of tens of millions of people, restructure agriculture, redirect trade routes, and transform language and education.

Even decades after it left, the institutional effects could remain.

So the more accurate statement was never:

The West did not invade other people.

Of course it did.

What needs correcting is this:

**The West did not become the world's colonial power because it possessed some unique instinct for aggression that everyone else lacked.**

**It became the world's colonial power because it was the first to acquire the capacity for global colonization.**

Over time, we mistook that difference in capacity for a difference in civilizational character.

---

## But Here Is the More Ironic Question

This is where the subject becomes genuinely interesting.

> - Why do we now think colonialism was wrong?
> - Why, after Britain ruled India, do we ask whether Indians consented?
> - Why, after Spain conquered the Americas, do we discuss whether Indigenous peoples had rights?
> - Why, when one country annexes another today, do we no longer write, as an ancient chronicler might, “the realm expanded by a thousand miles,” and treat the military outcome as the whole story?

**Because the way we think about war and conquest has changed.**

And that change is itself one of the most important transformations of modern civilization.

For most of human history, conquest was not something that required much explanation. A ruler defeated a neighboring state, occupied its territory, and enlarged his domain. That was usually understood first as a sign of strength. Chinese history spoke of great unification. Indian history spoke of dynastic expansion. The Middle East spoke of the rise and fall of empires. Europe was no different.

Ancient people were not blind to the cruelty of war, and there were always traditions that opposed indiscriminate killing. China had ideas of benevolent government. India had Ashoka's remorse after Kalinga. Christian and Islamic traditions both contained long discussions of mercy, justice, and limits in war.

But none of this fundamentally overturned one political logic that endured for a very long time:

**If I can conquer you, then I can rule you.**

**Victory itself was often a source of legitimacy.**

The truly dramatic change came later.

Individual liberty, natural rights, equality before the law, abolitionism, constitutionalism, national self-determination, universal human rights, and eventually a more systematic humanitarian tradition gradually changed the way political violence was judged.

From that point onward, a state could no longer simply say:

I defeated you, therefore this land is mine.

People kept asking:

> - By what right?
> - What happens to the people you conquered?
> - Do they have the right to determine their own future?
> - Even once war has begun, are there things the victor is still not allowed to do?

I think this was one of the genuinely important turning points of modern civilization.

## What Humanitarianism Changed Was the Way We Look at War

Modern civilization did not make war disappear.

There is still war.

There is still aggression.

There is still slaughter.

Humanitarianism did not “abolish war.” What it changed was something else:

**War stopped being only a question of who won.**

In older accounts of war, the first things we usually see are:

> Who won, who lost, how much territory changed hands, how many enemy soldiers were destroyed, how much larger the empire became.

Modern people add another set of questions:

> - How many civilians were killed?
> - Were prisoners abused?
> - Were children harmed?
> - Were the wounded treated humanely?
> - How far may a state go in order to win a war?
> - Are there things that remain forbidden even if they might help you win?

Today, this shift can seem completely ordinary.

It is not ordinary at all.

**For a very long part of human history, the massacre of a city after it refused to surrender, the enslavement of a defeated people, or the seizure of the defeated side's property was not something outside the recognizable logic of war.**

That was simply what war often meant.

The winner acquired the power to dispose of the loser.

Modern humanitarianism tried to build another rule:

**Even if you win, that does not mean you possess unlimited rights.**

The Red Cross movement, the Geneva Conventions, protections for prisoners of war, protections for civilians, war crimes, crimes against humanity, genocide—none of these concepts suddenly made the world humane.

Rules are violated every day.

But “a rule is violated” and “there is no such rule at all” are not the same thing.

At least at the level of principle, humanity began to recognize something different:

The enemy in war is still a human being.

The defeated are still human beings.

Civilians are not simply a cost to be calculated at will.

And I think this is precisely the part of Western civilization that is easiest to overlook today.

## What We Most Need to Reconsider About Western Civilization Is Not Only Science and Industry

When we say Western civilization changed the world, we usually think first of the Scientific Revolution, the Industrial Revolution, capitalism, modern medicine, the modern state, and technological progress.

But I increasingly think something deeper is often ignored:

**Western civilization helped change the way humanity understands power itself.**

The old logic was simple:

> - I am stronger than you, therefore I may conquer you.
> - My country has a stronger army, therefore I may decide your fate.
> - My ruler won the war, therefore he has the right to rule you.

That logic runs through enormous stretches of human history.

Modern political civilization gradually added another layer of restraint:

> - Having power does not mean you may use it however you want.
> - Winning a war does not mean you may do whatever you want to the defeated.
> - Possessing the machinery of the state does not mean the state may dispose of individuals at will.
> - Majority support does not mean minorities have no rights.
> - Having the capacity to hurt someone does not mean you have the right to hurt them.

This is, in fact, a deeply “unnatural” political ethic.

**The most natural human logic is often:**

**I can do it, therefore I do it.**

What modern civilization struggled to establish was another sentence:

**I can do it, but that does not mean I have the right to do it.**

I do not think this sentence is any less important than the steam engine, electricity, or modern medicine.

Technology answers the question of what human beings *can* do.

Civilization faces the harder question:

**Even if we can do it, should we still refuse to do it?**

## That Is Why I Do Not Accept Rewriting Western Civilization as a History of Sin

This is also why I find it difficult to accept the way some communist states, or parts of the radical left today, narrate Western civilization.

In that narrative, Western civilization is reduced almost entirely to a list of charges:

Colonialism, imperialism, slavery, racism, war, plunder.

All of those things happened.

Nothing in this article is intended to defend them.

**But if those are the only things we use to define a civilization, what we are looking at is no longer history. It is a selective prosecution.**

**Because if we use the same method, almost every civilization can be rewritten as a history of sin.**

China had war, massacre, and expansion.

India had imperial conquest.

The Islamic world had dynastic and sectarian wars.

Indigenous societies in the Americas also had empire, tribute, war, and conquest.

Europe hardly needs mentioning.

If the only criterion for judging a civilization is:

Did it ever harm other people?

Then almost no civilization in human history can survive the trial.

So the more important question is:

**Beyond repeating the violence humanity already knew, what else did a civilization create?**

The West pushed the capacity for conquest to an unprecedented level.

That is a historical responsibility it cannot escape.

But within the same civilization there also developed ideas of liberty, equality, individual rights, limits on state power, abolition, humanitarianism, the rule of law, and universal human rights.

And those ideas eventually became some of the most important weapons used to criticize colonialism, slavery, and imperialism themselves.

That is what I find genuinely interesting.

---

The West built vast colonial systems. But anti-colonial movements also drew heavily on the political language that developed in the modern West.

The West was deeply involved in the slave trade. But the modern abolitionist movement also grew there.

The West created unprecedented machinery of war. But modern humanitarian restraints on war, protections for prisoners, and protections for civilians also became institutionalized there.

The West pushed the capacity of the state to extraordinary levels. Then it began asking, again and again:

Where should the power of the state stop?

This is not the banal claim that “the West has both good and bad sides.”

That tells us very little.

What I am trying to say is:

**The most valuable quality of a civilization may not be that it never made terrible mistakes, but that it developed principles capable of judging itself, limiting itself, and even rejecting some of its own past behavior.**

That is a very different civilizational capacity.

## The Fact That We Condemn Colonialism Today Is Itself Part of a Civilizational Change

This creates a remarkable historical paradox.

The more we emphasize individual dignity, equality, human rights, and national self-determination today, the easier it becomes to look backward and discover how much of Western history was unacceptable by those standards.

Some people therefore conclude:

See? Western civilization itself was evil.

But I think that leaves out the most important step.

**What language are we using today to say those things were unacceptable?**

Why does a British person today also regard the slave trade as a moral crime?

Why do European countries argue over their colonial past?

Why must a modern state, even when it goes to war, constantly explain the legitimacy of what it is doing?

Why do civilian casualties become a political issue?

Why is the killing of prisoners of war treated as a war crime?

Because the moral standards by which war is judged have changed.

In other words:

**It is precisely the development of modern humanitarianism that gave us the ability to judge the past more severely.**

And that produces an apparently ironic result.

**The more Western civilization developed values centered on human rights, dignity, and equality, the more effectively we could use those values to criticize Western civilization's own history.**

**If we see only the later criticism, while forgetting how the standards behind that criticism were formed, then we understand only half of Western civilization.**

Of course, I am not saying modern human rights belong entirely to the West.

That would be just as simplistic.

Universal human rights became a global institution through participation from many civilizations. The drafters of the 1948 *Universal Declaration of Human Rights* came from different countries and cultural traditions, including P. C. Chang from China, Charles Malik from Lebanon, and participants from India and elsewhere.

But if we erase the European Enlightenment, liberalism, abolitionism, Christian humanism, constitutionalism, the rule of law, and the tradition of limiting state power, we still cannot explain how modern political civilization took shape.

So recognizing the value of Western civilization does not require denying its crimes.

Quite the opposite.

**A mature judgment of civilization should be able to hold both facts at the same time.**

## What We Have Really Misunderstood May Not Be the West, but Civilization Itself

I do not like either of the two extreme stories.

One story says:

The West gave the world science, liberty, and modern civilization, so the damage caused by colonialism can be ignored.

The other says:

Western power was fundamentally the result of colonialism, plunder, and oppression, so Western civilization itself is a kind of original sin that must be endlessly repented.

Both stories are wrong. Real history is much more troublesome.

Humanity already lived in a world full of war, competition, slavery, conquest, and expansion.

Indians conquered Indians.

Chinese conquered Chinese.

Muslim empires repeatedly fought one another.

Indigenous peoples in the Americas built empires and resisted empires built by other Indigenous peoples.

Europeans spent long stretches of their own history slaughtering one another.

Then, at a particular historical moment, the West moved first through a series of breakthroughs in science, finance, navigation, industry, military organization, and modern state capacity.

It suddenly acquired power far beyond that of other civilizations.

And then it did something human beings had always done, but on a scale never seen before:

**Conquest.**

Only this time, conquest became truly global.

So the West must bear responsibility for its history.

It possessed capacities others did not possess, and it used them.

But something else matters just as much. Modern civilization gradually developed a new set of principles:

- Even if you are overwhelmingly powerful, that does not give you unlimited rights.
- Even if you win a war, that does not make you inherently right.
- Even if a state possesses overwhelming military power, it cannot decide the fate of another people merely because it is capable of doing so.

This is the part of Western civilization that I want people to reconsider.

What deserves recognition in Western civilization is not that it never invaded anyone.

The more important point is:

**It also helped develop a system of values that taught humanity to ask whether conquest itself was legitimate.**

Both things happened within the same civilization.

That sounds contradictory.

But civilizations are contradictory.

They are not single personalities.

A civilization can build machines of war and also produce principles that restrain war.

It can build colonial empires and also give rise to anti-colonial thought.

It can participate in the slave trade and also generate abolitionist movements.

So if we look at the enormous wrongs committed by the West and therefore reduce Western civilization itself to a burden of guilt that should be rejected, I think we are still misreading history.

Because doing that makes us forget something even more important:

**Humanity did not walk into Western modernity from a peaceful, equal world in which everyone already respected everyone else.**

Quite the opposite.

**We came from a world that, for a very long time, treated war, conquest, hierarchy, and the domination of the weak by the strong as normal—and only gradually arrived where we are today.**

People still start wars.

They still invade.

They still kill.

But at least we have come to believe that such actions must be justified, constrained, and sometimes judged.

That is already a profound change.

So if we have to name one of the most important things Western civilization left to humanity, beyond science, industry, and the modern state, I would include a legacy that is often underestimated:

**It helped advance a civilizational principle: power does not automatically make you right.**

And the real significance of that sentence lies in one uncomfortable fact:

For most of the last several thousand years, human history often believed the opposite.
