In the previous article, I calculated the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate, or LCER, for a typical dual-income private-sector household in Shanghai.

The central estimate was:

**101.8%.**

What matters about that number is not simply that “China has a high tax burden.” If we looked only at personal income tax—or even at conventional taxes and social insurance contributions—it would be difficult to produce anything close to 100%.

What pushed the Shanghai model above 100% was a different chain:

> **land finance → high housing prices → long-term household leverage → financing costs → education competition and other institutional costs.**

That leads naturally to the next question: what happens if we apply exactly the same yardstick to the United States?

If the “Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate” were merely an indicator designed to describe China, it would not be particularly useful. The real test is to take the same methodology into a country with a fundamentally different institutional structure.

The household still consists of two ordinary workers. They still have one child. They still buy a home. Pension and healthcare benefits still have to be credited back. Ordinary consumption, the construction value of the home, and mortgage principal still do not count as extraction.

Then we ask:

**Over the course of a lifetime, how much of an ordinary American urban household’s labor value is transferred away through taxes, social insurance, housing institutions, housing finance, and education competition?**

The result is:

> **New York: 30.3%.**  
> **Chicago: 23.0%.**

Both are far below Shanghai.

But the interesting point is not that “America is only one-third of China.”

It is this:

**Institutional extraction exists in both countries, but wealth leaves the household balance sheet through completely different channels.**

That is the real question in the U.S. case.

------

## 1. The Same Yardstick, Applied to New York and Chicago

The previous article already explained in detail what LCER measures, so I will not rebuild the methodology from scratch here.

The definition remains the same:

> **LCER = present value of net institutional costs ÷ present value of household lifetime labor value.**

The denominator is not after-tax income.

It is:

**cash wages + legally required employer labor contributions.**

The numerator is not simply “government revenue.”

Pension contributions cannot all be treated as losses. Neither can healthcare contributions. Any pension, medical, or other benefit that can reasonably be attributed back to the household must be deducted.

Housing is treated the same way. A high home price does not mean the entire value of the home is an institutional cost. Materials, construction, normal land value, and the housing asset itself all represent real economic value.

So the housing component includes only:

**identifiable institutional premiums, property taxes, and transaction taxes.**

For mortgages, only interest is counted, not principal. For education, only spending above the common cross-country benchmark is treated as excess competitive expenditure.

In other words:

**the yardstick does not change.**

Only Shanghai is replaced by New York and Chicago.

New York serves as the **Leading City**. It is one of the largest and most important employment and economic centers in the United States, with high wages, expensive housing, New York State income tax, and New York City income tax.

Chicago is more suitable as the **Representative City**. It still has a large, complete private-sector labor and housing market, but both wages and home prices are substantially lower than in New York, making it closer to the upper-middle range of major American cities.

In 2021, BLS data put average annual pay across occupations at roughly **$74,150** in the New York metropolitan area and **$61,860** in Chicago. New York’s average hourly wage was about 27% above the national average; Chicago’s was only about 6% higher.

There is one geographic issue worth noting. Wage and housing data use the New York–Newark–Jersey City and Chicago–Naperville–Elgin metropolitan markets, while local income and property taxes are modeled as though the household actually lives in New York City or Chicago.

That is not a perfect match.

For that reason, I rate the confidence level for both cities as:

**Medium.**

## 2. How Much Labor Value Does an American Dual-Income Household Create Over a Lifetime?

The standard U.S. household still consists of two full-time workers who begin working at age 25, one man and one woman, with one child.

The major difference from the China model is retirement age.

For people born in 1960 or later, the Social Security full retirement age is 67. That gives a working life of:

**42 years.**

As before, I assume zero long-run real wage growth, a 2% real discount rate, and a home purchase in the tenth working year with a 30% down payment and 70% mortgage financing.

In New York, individual annual pay is:

**$74,150.**

Combined household cash wages are:

**$148,300.**

But cash wages are not the employer’s full statutory labor cost.

BLS data for September 2021 put private-sector wage costs at $26.36 per hour and legally required benefits at $2.80 per hour. These required benefits include Social Security, Medicare, unemployment insurance, and workers’ compensation.

Relative to cash wages, that is approximately:

**10.6%.**

So the New York household creates about:

**$164,053**

in labor value per year.

Discount all 42 working years back to age 25, and the present value of lifetime labor value is:

**$4,724,633.**

For Chicago:

Household cash wages:

**$123,720.**

Annual labor value:

**$136,862.**

Present value of lifetime labor value:

**$3,941,548.**

These are the denominators for everything that follows.

## 3. If We Look Only at Taxes and Social Insurance, New York Is Actually Higher Than Shanghai

Start with the most conventional items:

taxes, pensions, healthcare contributions, and consumption taxes.

The result is:

**New York: 22.5%.**

**Chicago: 16.0%.**

In the Shanghai model, the comparable combination of net social-insurance costs, personal income tax, VAT, and consumption taxes came to roughly:

**15.6%.**

So if the analysis stopped here, the conclusion would actually be:

> **The visible lifetime fiscal and social-insurance burden on a typical New York household is higher than in Shanghai.**

Chicago is almost at the same level as Shanghai.

This matters.

It tells us that Shanghai’s 101.8% result was never driven by “especially high taxes.”

The part that creates the order-of-magnitude difference between China and the United States has not appeared yet.

Start with Social Security.

In 2021, the U.S. OASDI payroll tax was 6.2% for the employee and 6.2% for the employer, for a combined rate of 12.4%. Neither representative wage in this model reaches the taxable earnings ceiling.

But the entire 12.4% cannot be treated as a loss.

Workers receive retirement benefits later.

The present value of lifetime Social Security contributions is approximately:

**$529,600** for New York,

and

**$441,821** for Chicago.

Benefits cannot be estimated using the national average retirement payment. They need to be derived from the benefit formula appropriate to the worker’s earnings history.

Social Security has a strongly progressive replacement structure: lower-income workers receive a higher benefit relative to prior earnings.

The resulting present value of future Social Security benefits for the couple is approximately:

**$382,845** in New York,

and

**$337,718** in Chicago.

That leaves a true net pension extraction of:

**$146,754** in New York,

and

**$104,103** in Chicago.

Relative to lifetime labor value, that is only about:

**3.1% and 2.6%, respectively.**

The retirement period also cannot be estimated by taking life expectancy at birth and subtracting 67.

The people in this model have already survived to 67.

The correct measure is remaining life expectancy conditional on reaching that age. SSA’s 2021 life table gives roughly 15.63 remaining years for a 67-year-old man and 18.23 years for a woman.

## 4. The Most Counterintuitive Result in the U.S. Model: Medicare Is Negative Extraction

If Social Security dramatically reduces the apparent burden implied by its 12.4% contribution rate, Medicare is even more interesting.

New York:

**−$44,675.**

Chicago:

**−$65,203.**

The negative sign means:

> **Under the methodology used here, the present value of Medicare benefits attributable to this household exceeds the present value of its direct Medicare payroll taxes and retirement premiums.**

In 2021, Medicare paid roughly $828.5 billion in benefits for about 63.8 million beneficiaries, implying average benefits of about $14,056 per beneficiary per year.

But Medicare is not a closed system financed only by the 2.9% payroll tax.

In 2021 it also received roughly:

**$405.4 billion in general revenue financing.**

That produces an interesting lifecycle result.

The New York household earns more, so it pays more Medicare payroll tax during its working years. In the model, the present value of healthcare contributions and retirement premiums is about:

**$152,973.**

The present value of future Medicare benefits is approximately:

**$197,647.**

The net result is therefore:

**−$44,675.**

The Chicago household earns less, so its contribution present value is only:

**$132,444.**

But because the model uses the same national Medicare benefit proxy, the future benefit present value remains roughly:

**$197,647.**

The net result becomes:

**−$65,203.**

This does not mean American healthcare is cheap.

It certainly does not mean American healthcare is free.

The cost of private healthcare for the working-age population is a separate and very large issue.

But that is not the question being asked here.

The question is:

**How much of that spending qualifies as compulsory institutional extraction under this methodology?**

In 2021, the United States did not have a universal federal penalty requiring every individual to purchase private health insurance. Private medical spending therefore cannot simply be inserted into LCER because American healthcare is expensive.

If we did that, the model would lose its boundaries.

The broader point is important:

> **LCER cannot be calculated by mechanically adding tax and contribution rates.**

Benefits have to be credited back.

Otherwise, it is not measurement.

It is just arithmetic.

------

## 5. What Really Separates New York from Chicago Is Income Tax

The largest difference between the two U.S. cities is not Social Security.

It is not Medicare.

It is:

**personal income tax.**

The present value of lifetime personal income taxes for the New York household is approximately:

**$842,804.**

For Chicago:

**$498,595.**

The gap is more than:

**$344,000.**

Part of that is simply because New York wages are higher.

But the larger institutional difference is that New York residents pay federal income tax, New York State income tax, and New York City personal income tax.

Chicago does not have a comparable municipal personal income tax.

Add sales taxes and excise taxes, and the net fiscal and social-insurance burden becomes:

| Item | New York | Chicago |
|---|---:|---:|
| Net Social Security extraction | $146,754 | $104,103 |
| Net Medicare extraction | **−$44,675** | **−$65,203** |
| Personal income tax | $842,804 | $498,595 |
| Sales tax | $106,774 | $78,388 |
| Excise tax | $12,813 | $14,252 |
| **Net fiscal/social extraction** | **$1,064,470** | **$630,134** |
| **Share of lifetime labor value** | **22.5%** | **16.0%** |

The defining characteristic of this part of the American system is obvious:

**it is visible.**

You see it on the paycheck.

You see it when you file your taxes.

You see it every year.

Housing is where the comparison with China begins to change completely.

## 6. The United States Has Institutional Housing Costs Too—but the Mechanism Is Different

The representative 2021 home price in New York is approximately:

**$561,000.**

In Chicago:

**$330,400.**

Relative to annual household cash wages, that is:

**3.78 times** in New York,

and

**2.67 times** in Chicago.

In the previous Shanghai model, an average commercial apartment cost roughly:

**33 years of the household’s pre-tax wages.**

New York is 3.78 years.

Chicago is 2.67.

These are not remotely the same order of magnitude.

But even here, a high American home price cannot simply be labeled institutional extraction.

The United States does not have China’s land-conveyance fiscal system. That does not mean housing supply is entirely determined by an unrestricted market.

The real question is:

**How much have local land-use rules raised the price of housing?**

Gyourko and Krimmel studied residential land markets across multiple U.S. metropolitan areas and estimated what they call a “zoning tax” created by supply restrictions.

The term can be misleading if read literally as a tax paid to government.

A better description is:

**a regulatory premium,**

or:

**scarcity rent created by restrictions on housing supply.**

The mechanism is roughly:

> **land-use restrictions  
> → new housing supply becomes harder to add  
> → developable land becomes scarcer  
> → scarcity is capitalized into land and housing prices  
> → homebuyers pay a higher purchase price.**

That is fundamentally different from the Shanghai mechanism:

> **government control of the primary urban land market  
> → land-conveyance revenue enters the fiscal system  
> → land prices enter development costs  
> → households pay through home prices.**

The mechanisms are different.

They merely share one possible final symptom: higher housing prices.

Converting the paper’s central estimates into 2021 dollars, the model uses:

New York:

**$164,473.**

Chicago:

**$68,356.**

But U.S. housing costs include another major institutional channel:

**property tax.**

And this produces one of the more interesting results in the entire U.S. model.

The final institutional housing extraction rate is:

New York:

**4.7%.**

Chicago:

**4.7%.**

Almost exactly the same.

At first this looks strange.

New York housing is much more expensive, and its zoning premium is also much higher. Why is Chicago not substantially lower?

Because Chicago’s effective property-tax burden is much higher.

The model uses approximately:

New York:

**0.545%.**

Chicago:

**1.444%.**

So New York’s burden is concentrated more heavily at:

**entry.**

Homes are more expensive, and the regulatory premium is larger.

Chicago bears a greater share of its burden during:

**long-term ownership.**

The home is much cheaper to buy, but the household continues to pay a higher property tax for decades.

Once a one-time purchase-price premium and decades of recurring ownership taxes are placed into the same lifecycle cash-flow model, both cities end up at roughly:

**4.7%.**

That reveals something important:

> **High housing prices do not automatically imply a high Housing Institutional LCER.**

And the reverse is also true:

> **Low housing prices do not automatically imply low institutional housing costs.**

The real question is:

**At which point in the housing lifecycle does the system charge the household?**

------

## 7. Housing Is Also the Largest Source of Uncertainty in the U.S. Model

This is where the model needs a clear warning.

The zoning premium is the largest source of uncertainty in the U.S. estimate, especially in Chicago.

The underlying research itself notes that much of the stronger Chicago zoning-tax evidence is concentrated closer to the metropolitan core. Outside the core, the evidence for similarly large premiums is much weaker.

So Chicago’s:

**$68,356**

should not be read as a precisely measured “true cost.”

It is better understood as:

**a central estimate inferred from the best available empirical evidence.**

To see how much this assumption matters, I ran an extreme sensitivity test.

I removed the zoning premium completely.

Not by 20%.

Not by half.

By:

**100%.**

New York’s LCER falls from 30.3% to:

**27.5%.**

Chicago falls from 23.0% to:

**21.6%.**

So the zoning premium matters.

But it is not the pillar supporting the entire conclusion.

Even if we remove it completely, the U.S. result remains broadly in the 20%–30% range.

It does not move anywhere close to Shanghai’s 100%.

------

## 8. What Creates the Order-of-Magnitude Housing Gap Between China and the United States Is Leverage After the Home Price Is Set

As in the previous article, mortgage principal does not count as extraction.

If you borrow $300,000, you also receive $300,000 in financing to purchase an asset.

The actual financing cost is:

**interest.**

For a representative $561,000 New York home, a 30% down payment leaves a mortgage of:

**$392,700.**

For a $330,400 Chicago home:

**$231,280.**

Using a representative 2021 U.S. 30-year fixed mortgage, counting only interest and discounting the payments back to age 25, the present value of mortgage interest is:

New York:

**$134,148.**

As a share of lifetime labor value:

**2.8%.**

Chicago:

**$79,006.**

Equivalent to:

**2.0%.**

Now place those figures next to the Shanghai result from the previous article:

**30.8%.**

The gap is already an order of magnitude.

This is not mainly because American banks are unusually generous.

The real reason is:

**the mortgage principal is not remotely on the same scale.**

The causal chain therefore remains:

> **the housing system first determines how much principal the household must finance  
> → principal determines how many years of financial services the household must purchase  
> → the interest rate then determines how expensive that financing becomes.**

The mortgage is not the first-order problem.

It is the mechanism that keeps collecting on the housing-price structure that came before it.

------

## 9. Education Is Not a Major Source of U.S. LCER

Education follows the same rule as before.

The fact that American college tuition is expensive does not mean the entire cost of educating a child should be classified as institutional extraction.

What this model measures is:

> **private education spending by an ordinary household that exceeds a common baseline and can reasonably be interpreted as competitive excess expenditure.**

Using Japan’s household education share of roughly 1.5% as the cross-country baseline, the U.S. calculation uses 2021 Consumer Expenditure Survey data for married couples with children.

Only the portion above the benchmark is counted, and only during the years in which education spending occurs.

The result is:

New York:

**about $13,587.**

Chicago:

**about $13,587.**

As a share of lifetime labor value:

**about 0.3%.**

The Shanghai figure from the previous article was:

**1.7%.**

This does not mean American education has no serious problems.

College tuition, student debt, school-district housing premiums, and elite-education competition are all substantial subjects in their own right.

It means only this:

**Under the common methodology used here, the excess competitive education spending that can currently be identified with reasonable confidence is not a major driver of U.S. LCER.**

If the evidence is not there, the number should not be inflated simply because a larger result feels more intuitive.

## 10. Putting It All Together: New York 30.3%, Chicago 23.0%

The central U.S. estimates can now be assembled.

| Item | New York | Chicago |
|---|---:|---:|
| Net fiscal/social extraction | **22.5%** | **16.0%** |
| Housing institutional cost | **4.7%** | **4.7%** |
| Mortgage interest | **2.8%** | **2.0%** |
| Excess education competition | **0.3%** | **0.3%** |
| Monopoly / other quantifiable institutional costs | **Not Included** | **Not Included** |
| **Core LCER** | **30.3%** | **23.0%** |
| **Total LCER** | **30.3%** | **23.0%** |

In other words:

**New York: 22.5 + 4.7 + 2.8 + 0.3 = 30.3%.**

**Chicago: 16.0 + 4.7 + 2.0 + 0.3 = 23.0%.**

In dollar terms, the New York household creates about:

**$4.72 million**

in lifetime labor value.

The identifiable institutional net costs included in the central estimate amount to roughly:

**$1.43 million.**

The Chicago household creates about:

**$3.94 million**

in lifetime labor value.

The corresponding cost is roughly:

**$0.91 million.**

So the correct interpretation is not:

“America has a 30% tax rate.”

It is:

> **Under the standard household assumptions used here, roughly 30% of the lifetime labor value created by a dual-income New York household becomes identifiable lifecycle institutional cost through net fiscal and social-insurance extraction, housing institutions, long-term housing finance, and excess education competition. In Chicago, the figure is about 23%.**

Those are two very different statements.

## 11. Put the United States Next to Shanghai, and the Real Difference Finally Appears

Now the comparison becomes meaningful.

The previous Shanghai central estimate was:

**101.8%.**

New York:

**30.3%.**

Chicago:

**23.0%.**

But if we summarize this as:

“China takes 100% and America takes 30%,”

we have misunderstood the model completely.

The real comparison is not the total.

It is:

**how the total is produced.**

Start with the conventional layer.

Shanghai’s net tax and social-insurance burden:

**about 15.6%.**

New York:

**22.5%.**

Chicago:

**16.0%.**

If we looked only at conventional taxation, New York would actually come out well above Shanghai, while Chicago would be almost identical.

The real separation appears in housing.

Shanghai’s land and real-estate fiscal chain:

**49.1%.**

New York’s housing institutional cost:

**4.7%.**

Chicago:

**4.7%.**

Then mortgage interest.

Shanghai:

**30.8%.**

New York:

**2.8%.**

Chicago:

**2.0%.**

At that point, the question is no longer:

**Which country has the higher tax rate?**

The real question becomes:

> **Does a system extract primarily from the household’s annual income flow, or through asset prices and long-term household debt?**

These are entirely different mechanisms of wealth transfer.

Much of the U.S. burden occurs in:

**income flows.**

You earn wages and pay federal income tax.

You pay state tax.

In New York, you also pay city income tax.

You contribute to Social Security and Medicare while working.

You pay sales and excise taxes when you consume.

The defining feature is:

**visibility.**

You can usually see where the money went.

The largest part of the Shanghai model works differently.

Land finance does not appear on the payslip with a line saying:

“Land-conveyance revenue deducted today.”

It enters the land price first.

The land price enters the home price.

The home price determines how much the household has to borrow.

The mortgage principal then determines decades of future interest payments.

And eventually, even the savings of the previous generation may be pulled into the down payment.

The chain becomes:

> **land institutions  
> → housing prices  
> → household balance sheets  
> → long-term leverage  
> → financing costs  
> → intergenerational wealth mobilization.**

That is why an ordinary Shanghai household can simultaneously feel:

“My personal income tax is not particularly high,”

and also discover:

“I am spending most of my working life paying for housing.”

There is no contradiction.

## 12. Could the U.S. Estimate of 23%–30% Still Be Too Low?

Possibly.

There are several real institutional costs that I know exist but have not included in the central estimate.

The first is sales and excise taxes during retirement. The state and local tax-incidence data used here primarily cover non-elderly households, so the model applies them only during the working years. Retirees continue to consume and pay sales taxes, which means this treatment probably creates some downward bias.

The second is development fees, impact fees, and permitting delays. These clearly can raise housing costs, but some portion is likely already capitalized into the zoning premium. Adding them again without a reliable decomposition risks counting the same supply restriction twice.

The third is unemployment insurance and workers’ compensation. Employer contributions to these systems are already included in lifetime labor value, but calculating net extraction would require estimating how often the standard household becomes unemployed, for how long, whether either worker is injured, and how much the household receives in benefits over 42 years.

There is not enough reliable lifecycle evidence to do that confidently.

So under the conservative rule used throughout the methodology, net extraction is temporarily set to zero.

The fourth category is government-protected monopoly and resource misallocation.

The United States obviously has protected industries, regulatory barriers, rent-seeking, and inefficient allocation.

But:

“these things exist”

and

“I can reliably convert them into the lifetime dollar loss borne by an ordinary household”

are not the same claim.

I have not found a sufficiently robust household-level counterfactual estimate that can also avoid double-counting taxes, housing regulation, and other components.

So the central estimate remains:

**Not Included.**

That does not mean:

**0 = nonexistent.**

There is also one particularly important time-specific issue:

**the 2021 Child Tax Credit.**

All countries in this project use 2021 as the common base year. That means the U.S. model has to use the actual 2021 tax system.

The problem is that the Child Tax Credit was temporarily expanded during the pandemic.

So if the 2021 tax structure is frozen and treated as representative across the household’s entire lifecycle, the U.S. personal-income-tax estimate will partly reflect a temporary policy.

That is not a calculation error.

The base year is 2021, so the model should use 2021.

But it does mean that a future cross-country database should probably keep two U.S. scenarios:

**a 2021 actual-policy scenario**

and

**a pre-ARPA / long-run normalized CTC scenario.**

What it should not do is quietly replace the current central estimate with a different year simply because the 2021 U.S. policy happened to be unusual.

If the methodology claims to use a common base year, that rule has to survive inconvenient cases as well.

Otherwise, “common methodology” eventually becomes manual adjustment.

## 13. What the U.S. Case Changed About How I Think About Extraction

When I wrote the Shanghai article, it was easy to come away with one impression:

Is the point of the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate simply to add land finance, mortgage costs, and other burdens that conventional tax statistics leave out?

After running the U.S. model, I no longer think that is the right way to understand it.

The real purpose is not:

**to add more costs.**

It is:

**to place the major flows of wealth between a household and the institutional system onto a single lifecycle balance sheet.**

The United States demonstrates why that matters.

If the model were designed merely to produce a larger number, Medicare would never show:

**−$44,675**

and

**−$65,203.**

But those numbers should be negative.

Benefits came back.

Likewise, if Chicago’s zoning premium is uncertain, the correct response is not to search for excuses to preserve it.

The right thing to do is to remove it and see what happens.

When it is removed, New York falls from 30.3% to 27.5%, and Chicago from 23.0% to 21.6%.

The conclusion survives.

So the zoning premium is an important parameter, but it is not the pillar on which the entire result rests.

A meaningful model cannot allow numbers to move only upward.

It must also allow them to move down.

------

What does New York’s 30.3% actually mean?

It does not mean:

“the U.S. government takes 30% of the household’s wages.”

Of course not.

The figure includes income taxes, net social-insurance extraction, zoning premiums, property taxes, and mortgage interest. Social Security and Medicare benefits are credited back.

What it means is:

> **For the representative dual-income New York homeowning household defined in this model, once identifiable net fiscal burdens, net social-insurance costs, institutional housing costs, housing-finance costs, and excess education competition are all converted into present values and compared with lifetime labor value, the central estimate is about 30%.**

For Chicago, it is about:

**23%.**

But the most important thing about the U.S. result is not the number 30.

It is that it makes Shanghai’s 100% easier to understand.

If we look only at conventional taxes, New York is actually higher than Shanghai.

What pushes the two models into completely different territory is:

**housing.**

More precisely:

> **whether an institutional system can capitalize a very large cost into an essential household asset, and then force the household to keep paying for that price through decades of financial leverage.**

The United States also restricts land.

It also levies property taxes.

It also has expensive housing.

It also has mortgage finance.

But in the two cities modeled here, that chain does not push ordinary home prices to the equivalent of several decades of a dual-income household’s wages.

So mortgage finance does not amplify the institutional cost by another order of magnitude.

The previous article ended with a question:

> **If a Chinese urban household creates 100 units of wealth across three generations, how much is ultimately transferred out of the household sector through various institutional channels?**

After the U.S. case, I think the question needs to go one step further.

The most useful cross-country comparison may no longer be:

**Which country has higher taxes?**

It may not even be:

**Which government takes more?**

The better question is:

> **At what point does a society charge ordinary households?**

Some systems charge on the payslip.

Some charge at the point of consumption.

Some collect through retirement systems and then return part of the money later.

Some charge through land.

Some capitalize institutional costs into housing prices.

Some then use debt to stretch today’s institutional price into thirty years of future cash flow.

And what ultimately determines how much freedom a household has over its own life is not the statutory tax rate.

It is what remains on the household balance sheet after all of those channels have run their course:

**how much of the labor value created over a lifetime actually stays with the household.**
