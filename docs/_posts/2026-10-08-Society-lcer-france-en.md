In the previous article, we calculated the United Kingdom.

The result was:

> - **London: 27.7%.**
> - **Manchester: 17.0%.**

Manchester's 17.0% surprised me a little. It was low enough that I found it difficult to believe, and I spent a great deal of time checking how much of what people paid into the British system later came back through pensions and the NHS.

So now let us look at France.

France has recently seen a new wave of large-scale student protests. The initial demands were not abstract slogans. They were very concrete problems: teacher shortages, overcrowded classes, school conditions and the university admissions system. In some places, violent clashes followed.[^student-protests]

That raises a natural question. In a country with such a high tax burden and such a large welfare state, why can shortages in basic public education still become a reason for students to take to the streets? Does this have anything to do with France's LCER[^lcer]?

So what does France look like?

---

If I relied only on intuition, I would have expected France to be easier to calculate than the UK.

Because the image of France is unusually clear:

- High taxes
- High social contributions
- High welfare

According to the OECD, France's conventional labor tax wedge[^tax-wedge] in 2021 was about **44.1%** for a married couple with two children where both spouses earned the average wage. Even when one spouse earned the average wage and the other earned 67% of the average wage, it was still **40.9%**.

So before seeing the result, I would probably have guessed:

France should have a very high Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate.

But the final calculation is:

> - **Paris: 34.0%.**
> - **Lyon: 27.8%.**

Paris at 34.0% is indeed high. It is slightly above New York's 30.3% and almost identical to Toronto's 34.1%.

But if France's conventional tax wedge is already around 40%, another question immediately appears:

**Why does the final LCER come out at only 34.0% and 27.8%?**

This time, the real question is no longer "why are French taxes so high?"

It is:

> **After France takes so much out of labor, how much of that money actually leaves the household for good, and how much later comes back through pensions and healthcare?**

The gap between those two numbers is what makes France interesting.

## 1. The Same Household, Just Across the Channel from Britain to France

The first four articles have already explained the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate in detail, so I will not reintroduce the whole model here.

It is still the same standard household:

Two adults, both starting work at age 25, one child; a 2% real discount rate[^discount-rate], 0% long-run real wage growth; buying a home in the 10th year of their careers, with a 30% down payment and a 70% mortgage.

The French model uses a normal pension age of 66, giving a 41-year working life.

Paris remains the Leading City[^city-type].

Lyon is the Representative City.

In 2021, the full-time-equivalent net wage in the private sector in the Paris metropolitan area was about **€3,222 per month**. Because there is no perfectly comparable city-level wage series for Lyon, I use **€2,490 per month** as a proxy, based on the category of metropolitan areas with more than 700,000 residents, including Lyon, Toulouse and Grenoble.

Using the same year's gross/net wage structure for France, the model produces approximate individual annual wages of:

- Paris: **€50,203**
- Lyon: **€38,721**

For a standard two-earner household, annual cash wages are therefore:

- Paris: **€100,407**
- Lyon: **€77,443**

The underlying wage data mainly come from [Insee](https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/8182274).

But as in the previous countries, cash wages are not the denominator of LCER.

If a French company pays an employee €100 in wages, its cost is not just €100. It must also pay substantial mandatory social contributions and other employer-side payroll levies on top.

After including all of these, the annual labor value of the Paris household is about:

> **€143,270.**

For Lyon:

> **€107,072.**

Discounted over 41 years back to age 25, lifetime labor value PV[^pv] is:

| Item                                 |          Paris |           Lyon |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------: | -------------: |
| Individual annual wage               |        €50,203 |        €38,721 |
| Household annual cash wage           |       €100,407 |        €77,443 |
| Employer mandatory contribution rate |          42.7% |          38.3% |
| Annual labor value                   |       €143,270 |       €107,072 |
| **Lifetime labor value PV**          | **€4,062,503** | **€3,036,080** |

These two numbers are the denominators for everything that follows.

## 2. In France, the Heavy Part Starts Before the Wage Is Even Paid

One important difference between France and the countries we have looked at before is that a large share of its institutional cost appears before the employee ever receives the wage.

Once a company decides to hire someone, the cost has already begun.

People are familiar with pensions, healthcare and unemployment insurance.

But French employers also face many mandatory levies that do not map neatly onto a future personal benefit account for the household.

In the OECD's 2021 wage-tax data, for example, contributions for construction, housing, apprenticeship and further training together amounted to around **2.646%** of wages.

Then there is something particularly French:

**versement mobilité[^versement-mobilite].**

Eligible employers pay this local charge as a percentage of payroll, and the money is mainly used to fund public transport.

In Paris, the 2021 rate was about:

> **2.95%.**

In Lyon:

> **1.85%.**

This money does not simply vanish. Training, housing policy and public transport can all create public value.

But LCER is not asking:

"Does this government spending have social value?"

It is asking:

**Does it create an equivalent personal asset or lifetime benefit that can reasonably be attributed to this household?**

If not, we cannot pretend the mandatory labor cost does not exist simply because it was paid on the employer side.

This is one of the easiest ways to underestimate France.

When we look at our own payslips, we tend to think:

My wage is my income; employer contributions are the company's money.

From a household balance-sheet perspective, that distinction is not very meaningful.

What the company is willing to spend each year to employ you is the market value of that labor.

If the system requires part of that value to be diverted before the wage reaches the employee's account, it is still part of labor value.

The employee simply never sees it.

## 3. But Pension Contributions Cannot All Be Counted as "Taken Away"

If the calculation stopped here, France would of course become a country with an extremely high extraction rate.

But that would be wrong.

French pension contributions are high, but pensions also genuinely come back in retirement.

In the OECD's 2021 model, the gross replacement rate[^replacement-rate] for a standard French worker under mandatory pension schemes was approximately:

- 0.5× average wage: **60.2%**
- 1× average wage: **60.2%**
- 2× average wage: **51.9%**

So France is not simply taking a large amount in pension contributions and then giving nothing back later.

Under the same lifecycle framework used throughout this series:

> **Net pension extraction = lifetime mandatory pension contribution PV − pension benefit PV attributable to the two spouses.**

### Paris

The Paris household's lifetime pension contribution PV is about **€794,531**.

The pension benefit PV attributable to the two spouses in retirement is about **€403,373**.

So the amount that actually enters LCER is not €794,531, but:

> **€391,158.**

### Lyon Works the Same Way

Contribution PV: **€613,693.**

Pension benefit PV: **€321,041.**

Final net pension extraction:

> **€292,652.**

---

This also shows one difference between France and the UK in the previous article.

Manchester incomes are clearly lower than London incomes, but the full State Pension does not fall in proportion to the wage gap between the two cities.

So Manchester receives a much larger relative benefit from pensions.

French pensions are more closely tied to career earnings and contribution records.

Paris pays more and receives more in retirement.

Lyon pays less, and future pension benefits also fall.

The benefits do come back.

But pensions do not erase the income gap between the two cities.

## 4. Healthcare Is the Same: You Cannot Count Only What Goes Out

French public healthcare creates the same kind of potential misunderstanding.

If we count healthcare contributions but ignore the medical services a household receives over an entire lifetime, France's extraction rate will obviously be overstated.

So healthcare follows exactly the same principle as in the previous articles:

**Contributions minus benefits.**

According to France's DREES data for 2021, average patient healthcare expenditure was about €2,900, of which AMO[^amo] reimbursed roughly €2,380, or about 82%.

At the same time, medical spending is not evenly distributed over a person's life.

It is lower when people are young.

It rises substantially with age.

So the model still uses an age-adjusted lifecycle healthcare-benefit profile rather than simply multiplying €2,380 by several decades.

The result is:

### Paris

Healthcare contribution PV: **€409,811.**

Lifetime healthcare benefit PV attributable to the household: **€187,209.**

Net healthcare extraction:

> **€222,603.**

### Lyon

Healthcare contribution PV: **€245,409.**

Healthcare benefit PV remains about: **€187,209.**

Net healthcare extraction:

> **€58,200.**

The difference is interesting.

The two cities use the same French public healthcare system, and the household age profile in the model is the same. So lifetime healthcare benefits do not change dramatically just because the family lives in Paris rather than Lyon.

But wages are different, which means the labor-side healthcare contribution cost is different.

The higher the household income, the larger the net difference.

This is one reason Paris ends up clearly above Lyon.

## 5. So France's 31.2% Is Not Obtained by Simply Adding Tax Rates

Putting pensions, healthcare, other mandatory employer levies, personal income tax, VAT and excise taxes back into one table gives:

| Fiscal and social-security item                        |                  Paris |                 Lyon |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------: | -------------------: |
| Pension contribution PV                                |               €794,531 |             €613,693 |
| Pension benefit PV                                     |              −€403,373 |            −€321,041 |
| **Net pension extraction**                             |           **€391,158** |         **€292,652** |
| Healthcare contribution PV                             |               €409,811 |             €245,409 |
| Healthcare benefit PV                                  |              −€187,209 |            −€187,209 |
| **Net healthcare extraction**                          |           **€222,603** |          **€58,200** |
| Social-debt[^social-debt] and other non-benefit levies |                €30,770 |              €23,733 |
| Other mandatory employer payroll levies                |               €159,323 |              €98,729 |
| Personal income tax PV                                 |               €264,247 |             €120,285 |
| VAT incidence PV[^vat-incidence]                       |               €147,380 |             €135,311 |
| Excise-tax PV                                          |                €50,189 |              €47,529 |
| **Net fiscal/social-insurance extraction**             | **€1,265,670 / 31.2%** | **€776,438 / 25.6%** |

At this point, the most important feature of France has already appeared.

Paris has not yet included housing.

It has not included mortgage interest.

It has not included education competition.

**Fiscal and social-security extraction alone already reaches 31.2%.**

Lyon is still at 25.6%.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-france-the-government-took-the-money-02.jpg" alt="ai-france-the-government-took-the-money-02" /></figure>

This is completely different from China.

In the China article, what pushed LCER so high was not personal income tax or social insurance, but land, housing and housing finance.

A large part of Toronto's result in Canada also comes from housing.

London's fiscal and social-insurance extraction is 20.5%, but mortgage repricing adds another 4.9 percentage points.

France is not like that.

The heaviest part in France happens directly in:

**Labor itself.**

A household creates labor value. The employer pays the cost. Wages are formed. Then pensions, healthcare, income tax, consumption taxes and various employer-side mandatory levies move layer by layer into the public system.

Some of it comes back through pensions and healthcare.

Even after deducting those returns, Paris is still left with 31.2%.

That is what France's high tax burden looks like when translated into LCER.

## 6. Housing in Paris Is Expensive, but I Still Do Not Count "Expensive" Itself

Then comes housing.

In 2021, an older apartment in Paris cost about:

> **€10,600/m².**

For an 80 m² home:

> **€848,000.**

Relative to household annual cash wages of €100,407, that is a price-to-income ratio of about:

> **8.45×.**

The median price of an older apartment in Lyon in Q4 2021 was about:

> **€5,090/m².**

The same 80 m²:

> **€407,200.**

Relative to household annual wages of €77,443:

> **5.26×.**

Housing in Paris is obviously not cheap.

Existing research also supports the view that housing supply elasticity in major French cities is relatively low, with land-use and planning regulation among the important reasons.

So should part of Paris's high home prices be counted as institutional extraction, as we did in China?

Not yet.

The reason is the same as in the London article.

I can say with reasonable confidence:

> **Planning and land-use restrictions reduce housing supply elasticity and capitalize part of demand growth into higher prices.**

But the question the model needs to answer is more specific:

> **Of a €848,000 home in Paris in 2021, how many euros were an institutional premium created by planning and supply restrictions?**

10%?

20%?

30%?

At the moment, I do not have a city-level counterfactual estimate that I consider reliable enough to answer that question directly.

So the treatment is simple:

> **Planning / supply restriction premium[^planning-premium]: Not Included.**

This does not mean France does not have the problem.

Nor does it mean Paris housing is reasonably priced.

It simply means I am not counting it yet.

If a model sees an 8.45× price-to-income ratio in Paris, decides first that 30% of the price must be an institutional cost, and then looks for reasons afterward, it is no longer measurement.

It is just turning a prior opinion into an Excel spreadsheet.

## 7. The Housing Costs We Can Clearly Identify Are Actually Not Very Large

For housing, there are currently two categories of cost that can be identified with reasonable confidence:

Public transaction charges at purchase, and recurring property tax during ownership.

For older homes in France, the combined DMTO[^dmto] rate commonly reaches about **5.81%**, plus a 0.10% contribution de sécurité immobilière, or CSI[^csi].

The purchase-tax PV in the model is therefore approximately:

- Paris: **€41,113**
- Lyon: **€19,742**

Then there is the taxe foncière[^taxe-fonciere].

Here I do not use "home price × an assumed tax rate."

That is because the French taxe foncière is not calculated simply by multiplying current market value by a uniform percentage.

According to actual DGFiP data for 2021, the average tax on apartments nationwide was around €760; the average for residential property in Paris was around €700, while Rhône was about €798.

So using actual tax amounts as a proxy is more appropriate.

Over the full lifecycle:

- Paris recurring property-tax PV: about **€19,526**
- Lyon: about **€21,915**

Somewhat counterintuitively, Lyon is slightly higher.

The total institutional housing cost is therefore:

> **Paris: about €60,640, or roughly 1.4% of lifetime labor value.**

> **Lyon: about €41,657, or roughly 1.3%.**

So although a Paris home costs more than twice as much as a Lyon home, the institutional housing-extraction rate that can currently be identified with confidence is almost the same.

There is no contradiction.

Because LCER has never asked:

**"Is this city expensive to live in?"**

It asks:

**"Of these costs, how much can be clearly attributed to institutions and does not become an equivalent asset owned by the household?"**

After buying that €848,000 home in Paris, the household still owns a home worth around €848,000.

A high house price does not mean €848,000 has disappeared.

## 8. French Mortgage Costs Are Actually Quite Light Compared with the Other Countries

French housing also differs sharply from the UK in another way.

In 2021, almost all newly issued French home loans were fixed-rate.

According to ACPR[^acpr], the share of fixed-rate home loans reached:

> **99.4%.**

Mortgage rates that year fell from about 1.24% to around 1.10% by year-end.

The model uses:

> **1.15%.**

The same home-purchase assumptions used throughout the series still apply:

- 30% down payment
- 70% mortgage

Loan amounts:

- Paris initial mortgage: **€593,600**
- Lyon: **€285,040**

Discounted lifetime mortgage interest is:

- Paris: **€55,025**
- Lyon: **€26,422**

Converted into LCER:

> **Paris: 1.35%.**

> **Lyon: 0.87%.**

This creates an interesting contrast with London.

When a London buyer purchased a home in 2021, a five-year fixed mortgage rate was also extremely low.

The problem was that the loan had to be repriced five years later.

By 2026, the low-rate era was over.

So London's lifetime mortgage cost was pushed back up to 4.9% of LCER.

French buyers in 2021 faced a different financing structure.

Once a fixed rate was signed, the risk of higher interest rates did not return to the household cash flow a few years later in the same way it did in Britain.

So even though housing in Paris is expensive, mortgage financing itself contributes only 1.35% to LCER.

That tells us at least one thing:

**The same "high house price" can leave households with very different long-term costs under different housing-finance systems.**

## 9. I Still Did Not Force Education and Other "Expensive France" Items into the Model

France certainly has education competition.

It has elite schools, school districts, extracurricular spending and competition around the Grandes Écoles[^grandes-ecoles].

But none of those facts automatically answers this question:

> "How much private education spending over the lifetime of an ordinary one-child French household is an additional cost created by institutional competition?"

At present, I do not have sufficiently stable data to put this item into the central model.

So:

> **Excess Education Competition: 0.0% / Not separately included.**

The same applies to other possible institutional costs.

Paris planning restrictions are one example.

They exist.

But they are not yet included in the model.

French pensions may also remain subject in retirement to CSG, CRDS and CASA[^retirement-levies], and pension income itself may be subject to personal income tax.

That means the pension benefit actually received by the household may be lower than the gross pension benefit used here.

But the countries already completed in this series have not consistently added retirement-stage pension taxation back into LCER.

So I do not change the rule only for France.

Otherwise, we would no longer be comparing countries with the same ruler.

If this is revised later, it should be revised for every country together.

## 10. Now Put the French Numbers Together

### Paris

Net fiscal and social-insurance extraction: **31.2%.**

Institutional housing cost: **1.4%.**

Mortgage interest: **1.4%.**

Education, housing-planning premium and other institutional costs that cannot yet be reliably monetized:

**Not Included.**

Final:

> **34.0%.**

### Lyon

Net fiscal and social-insurance extraction: **25.6%.**

Institutional housing cost: **1.3%.**

Mortgage interest: **0.9%.**

Other items that cannot yet be reliably monetized:

**Not Included.**

Final:

> **27.8%.**

The complete central result is:

| Item                                          |        Paris |         Lyon |
| --------------------------------------------- | -----------: | -----------: |
| Net fiscal/social-insurance extraction        |    **31.2%** |    **25.6%** |
| Institutional housing cost                    |     **1.4%** |     **1.3%** |
| Mortgage interest                             |     **1.4%** |     **0.9%** |
| Excess education competition                  |     **0.0%** |     **0.0%** |
| Housing planning / supply-restriction premium | Not Included | Not Included |
| Other institutional costs                     | Not Included | Not Included |
| **Core LCER**                                 |    **34.0%** |    **27.8%** |

In money terms:

The Paris household creates lifetime labor value with a present value of about:

> **€4.06 million.**

The identifiable net institutional lifecycle cost currently included in the model is about:

> **€1.38 million.**

The Lyon household creates lifetime labor value of about:

> **€3.04 million.**

Its corresponding net institutional lifecycle cost is about:

> **€844,000.**

That is the French result.

## 11. Put France Next to the Previous Four Countries, and a Fourth Structure Begins to Appear

The series now covers five countries.

| Country        | City       | Type                |       LCER |
| -------------- | ---------- | ------------------- | ---------: |
| China          | Shanghai   | Leading City        | **101.8%** |
| China          | Nanjing    | Representative City |  **75.3%** |
| United States  | New York   | Leading City        |  **30.3%** |
| United States  | Chicago    | Representative City |  **23.0%** |
| Canada         | Toronto    | Leading City        |  **34.1%** |
| Canada         | Winnipeg   | Representative City |  **26.7%** |
| United Kingdom | London     | Leading City        |  **27.7%** |
| United Kingdom | Manchester | Representative City |  **17.0%** |
| France         | Paris      | Leading City        |  **34.0%** |
| France         | Lyon       | Representative City |  **27.8%** |

One thing becomes obvious from the chart:

- Toronto is 34.1%.
- Paris is 34.0%.

Almost identical.

But the two 34% figures are built in completely different ways.

Housing is what really pushes Toronto upward.

Its net fiscal and social-insurance extraction is only 19.6%. Much of the rest comes from the institutional cost of housing and the financing cost required to enter a successful city.

Paris is the opposite.

Its institutional housing cost is only 1.4%, and mortgage interest is also only 1.4%.

The heavy part comes earlier:

> **31.2% net fiscal and social-security extraction.**

The same 34% can therefore sit in two very different places: one more on the household balance sheet, the other more in the process by which labor income is created and distributed.

New York and London are different again.

The more countries I calculate, the less the model seems to be answering a simple question like:

"Which country taxes the most?"

That question is starting to look too crude.

## 12. What Is Really High in France Is Not Just the Tax Rate, but the Share of Labor Value Absorbed by the Public System

The question at the beginning of the France article was:

If the conventional tax wedge is already above 40%, why does the final LCER come out at only 34.0% and 27.8%?

The answer is now fairly clear.

That roughly 40% is not all wealth permanently lost by the household.

Some of it is pensions.

Some of it is healthcare.

Those benefits come back later.

If they can reasonably be attributed to the household, they have to be deducted.

Otherwise, high-welfare countries would automatically be penalized by LCER while the benefits themselves were treated as if they did not exist.

But the opposite conclusion is also wrong:

"French taxes are high only because welfare is good, so the cost is not really that large."

Even after deducting those benefits, Paris still has 31.2% of net fiscal and social-insurance extraction.

Lyon still has 25.6%.

That is already a very heavy fiscal and social-security structure among the five countries calculated so far.

What makes France different may be precisely this:

It does not, like China, place most of the institutional cost onto household balance sheets through land and housing.

Nor does it, like Toronto, make the housing ticket into a successful city the main source of the urban gap.

France takes a large share first, while labor value is being formed.

The public system then returns part of it through pensions, healthcare and other public services.

---

So it is fair to say that the French government may also be one of the most adept governments in the developed world at "plucking the goose."

A large share of the tax burden is not collected only after money has entered the household's account. It is already taken during the formation of labor value, through employer contributions, social insurance and various payroll-related levies.

**A great deal of money is collected by the government before ordinary people ever actually receive it.**

France's recent student protests obviously cannot be attributed directly to LCER. The immediate reasons students took to the streets were teacher shortages, overcrowded classes, school conditions and the admissions system.[^student-protests]

But putting the two things side by side still creates a contrast that is difficult to ignore: a government can collect so much money before labor value reaches households, while users of some of the most basic public services still feel that resources are inadequate.

LCER does not explain the protests themselves, but it does make one question harder to avoid:

**After the money is collected, where does it actually go?**

---

[^lcer]: **LCER (Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate)** is the measure used in this series to estimate how much of a household's labor value is transferred away through identifiable institutional costs over the full lifecycle. It is calculated as: **present value of net institutional costs ÷ present value of household lifetime labor value**. Unlike a conventional tax rate, LCER can include not only taxes and social insurance, but also housing institutions, financing and other quantifiable channels, while pension, healthcare and other benefits attributable to the household must be deducted.

[^student-protests]: **2026 French student protests**: From mid-September 2026, high-school students in multiple parts of France launched protests that spread quickly. Public reporting cited shortages of teachers and substitute teachers, overcrowded classes, poor school conditions, timetable pressure and the Parcoursup admissions platform among the grievances. Arson, property damage and clashes with police later occurred in some areas, but those violent incidents should not be treated as equivalent to the demands of all students, nor can they be explained by LCER alone.

[^tax-wedge]: **Tax wedge**: A standard OECD measure of the tax burden on labor. It measures the gap between an employer's total labor cost and the employee's actual take-home income, usually including personal income tax and both employee and employer social-insurance contributions. It is an annual tax-burden measure and is not the same as the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate (LCER) calculated here.

[^discount-rate]: **Real discount rate**: The rate used to convert future income, contributions, benefits and costs into values at a common point in time. This article uses a 2% real discount rate, meaning the time value after removing inflation.

[^pv]: **PV (Present Value)**: The value today of income, contributions, benefits or costs occurring in different future years, discounted at a specified rate. In this article, all values are discounted back to the point when the household begins work at age 25.

[^city-type]: **Leading City / Representative City**: This series uses a "Leading City" and a "Representative City" to observe household burdens at different positions within the same national urban system. The Leading City is usually a core city where incomes, housing prices and economic activity are concentrated. The Representative City is a major city that is more representative of the broader national urban system, rather than the cheapest or poorest location.

[^versement-mobilite]: **Versement mobilité**: A local charge paid by qualifying French employers as a percentage of payroll, mainly to fund local public-transport construction and operations. Rates vary by region, which is why Paris and Lyon have different rates.

[^replacement-rate]: **Gross replacement rate**: Pension income after retirement as a percentage of pre-retirement labor income. "Gross" means before any income tax or social levies that may still apply during retirement.

[^amo]: **AMO (Assurance Maladie Obligatoire)**: France's statutory basic health-insurance system. The AMO reimbursements discussed here are medical costs actually covered by the public basic health-insurance system; they are not the same as cash income received by the household.

[^vat-incidence]: **VAT incidence**: This is not calculated simply by multiplying household consumption by France's standard 20% VAT rate. It estimates the VAT burden actually borne by the household after accounting for different product rates, consumption patterns and where the tax burden ultimately falls.

[^social-debt]: **Social-debt levy**: Some mandatory French levies do not directly create an individual pension or healthcare account. Instead, they help finance the social-security system and repay historical social debt. The portion that does not create an equivalent benefit attributable to the household is counted here as net institutional cost.

[^planning-premium]: **Planning / supply restriction premium**: If land-use rules, planning approval or supply restrictions reduce housing-supply elasticity, part of housing demand may be capitalized into higher home prices. This article accepts that the mechanism may exist, but does not include it directly in France's LCER because there is no sufficiently reliable city-level counterfactual estimate for 2021.

[^dmto]: **DMTO (Droits de mutation à titre onéreux)**: A group of transaction taxes and charges imposed on paid transfers of French real estate. For older homes, they usually consist of charges collected at different levels of local government. This article treats them as an institutional transaction cost because they do not become part of the housing asset owned by the household.

[^csi]: **CSI (Contribution de sécurité immobilière)**: A public charge associated with French real-estate registration, generally assessed as a percentage of the transaction value. The model uses 0.10%.
[^taxe-fonciere]: **Taxe foncière**: An annual local property tax paid by French property owners. Its tax base is not simply the property's current market price, so this article does not estimate it as "home price × a uniform tax rate." Actual tax amounts are used as a proxy instead.

[^acpr]: **ACPR (Autorité de contrôle prudentiel et de résolution)**: France's Prudential Supervision and Resolution Authority, part of the Banque de France system, responsible for prudential supervision of banks, insurers and other financial institutions. The mortgage-rate structure used in this article comes from its housing-finance statistics.

[^grandes-ecoles]: **Grandes Écoles**: A highly selective group of elite institutions within French higher education, operating alongside the ordinary university system and often admitting students through preparatory programs and competitive examinations. They clearly generate educational competition, but that does not mean all related household spending can automatically be counted in LCER.

[^retirement-levies]: **CSG, CRDS and CASA**: Social levies that may apply to pension income in France. CSG is the general social contribution; CRDS is mainly used to repay social-security debt; CASA supports spending related to elderly autonomy. Rates vary by income. This article does not separately add retirement-stage taxation to LCER, in order to keep the methodology consistent with the countries calculated earlier.
