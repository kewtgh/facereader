In the previous article, I applied the same **Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate** framework to the United States.

The result was:

**New York: 30.3%.**

**Chicago: 23.0%.**

What made that result interesting was not that “America is only one-third of China.”

It was that the same thing we call **institutional cost** reaches ordinary households through completely different channels.

In Shanghai, the heaviest burden sits in land, housing prices, and mortgage leverage.

In the United States, a larger share appears directly in income flows: income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and sales taxes.

So what happens in Canada?

For many people, the first image of Canada is simple:

- high taxes;
- generous welfare;
- universal healthcare.

So if a Canadian household ends up with a high Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate, the easy explanation is:

**It is a welfare state. Of course the taxes are high.**

But after running the numbers, that is not the most interesting part at all.

Using exactly the same framework as in the previous two articles:

> **Toronto: 34.1%.**
>
> **Winnipeg: 26.7%.**

If I compress the two cities into a simple country-level summary for cross-country comparison, the equal-weight average is:

> **30.4%.**

But 30.4% is not the most important number in this article.

What matters is the gap between Toronto and Winnipeg:

**7.4 percentage points.**

Because if we look only at conventional fiscal and social-insurance extraction, the result actually goes in the opposite direction:

- Toronto: **19.6%.**
- Winnipeg: **20.6%.**

In other words:

**Winnipeg carries the higher net tax and social-insurance burden, yet its final LCER is substantially lower than Toronto’s.**

Where do those 7.4 percentage points come from?

Almost entirely from one place:

**housing.**

So the real question in Canada is no longer:

> Are Canadian taxes high?

It is:

> **Why, within the same country, under the same pension system and the same public healthcare framework, does entering a successful city become so expensive?**

------

## 1. The same ruler, only now New York and Chicago become Toronto and Winnipeg

The previous two articles already explained the LCER framework in detail, so I will not rebuild the methodology from scratch here.

The core formula is still:

> **Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate = Present Value of Net Institutional Costs ÷ Present Value of Household Lifetime Labor Value**

Pension contributions cannot be counted without deducting future pension benefits.

Public healthcare cannot simply be treated as a cash benefit just because the government spends money on it.

And an expensive house cannot be counted as if the entire purchase price were an institutional extraction.

A house contains real construction value.

Land contains legitimate locational value.

Mortgage principal ultimately turns into household equity.

So in housing, I only count what can reasonably be identified as:

**institutional supply premium, transaction taxes, recurring property tax, and mortgage financing cost.**

Education is treated the same way as before: only private education spending above the common international benchmark is counted.

For Canada, I use two cities:

- Leading City: **Toronto**
- Representative City: **Winnipeg**

Toronto is one of Canada’s most important centers of employment, finance, and high-productivity activity.

Winnipeg has a complete labor and housing market, but without Toronto’s extreme housing valuation, Ottawa’s public-sector distortion, or Calgary’s resource-sector wage profile.

Put together, the two cities form an unusually useful contrast.

In 2021, median employment income for full-year, full-time workers was:

- Toronto: **CAD 69,500**
- Winnipeg: **CAD 58,000**

So for the standard two-earner household, annual cash wages are:

- Toronto: **CAD 139,000**
- Winnipeg: **CAD 116,000**

The source is the 2021 Canadian Census.

------

## 2. How much labor value does a two-earner Canadian household create over a lifetime?

The standard Canadian household is the same as before:

two full-time workers starting at age 25, one man and one woman, with one child.

Retirement age is set at 65.

So the working life is:

**40 years.**

The real discount rate remains 2%.

Long-run real wage growth remains 0%.

The household buys a home in year 10, with a 30% down payment and a 70% mortgage.

But as in the earlier articles, the denominator is not just cash wages.

An employer also has to pay mandatory labor costs such as CPP, EI, and workers’ compensation.

In 2021, CPP required both employee and employer contributions of 5.45%. EI also imposed mandatory employer contributions, while Ontario and Manitoba had their own WSIB/WCB costs.

Once these are included:

Toronto household annual labor value is approximately:

**CAD 149,728.**

Discounted over 40 years back to age 25:

> **CAD 4.178 million.**

Winnipeg:

annual labor value is approximately:

**CAD 125,533.**

Present value of lifetime labor value:

> **CAD 3.503 million.**

These two numbers are the denominators for everything that follows.

## 3. If we only look at taxes and social insurance, Winnipeg is actually heavier

Start with the most conventional layer:

taxes, pensions, and consumption taxes.

The result is:

| Item | Toronto | Winnipeg |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net pension extraction | −2.1% | −2.2% |
| Personal income tax, net | CAD 705,514 | CAD 614,633 |
| General sales-tax incidence | CAD 137,323 | CAD 126,873 |
| Excise / consumption tax | CAD 62,728 | CAD 57,954 |
| **Net fiscal / social extraction** | **19.6%** | **20.6%** |

The first thing that usually surprises people is the pension line.

It is negative.

For Toronto, the present value of lifetime pension contributions is approximately:

CAD 353,409.

The present value of pension benefits attributable to the couple is approximately:

CAD 440,193.

So the net result is:

**−CAD 86,784.**

Winnipeg is similar.

Contribution PV:

CAD 331,511.

Benefit PV:

CAD 407,866.

Net:

**−CAD 76,356.**

The minus sign does not mean the Canadian government is “paying people free money.”

It means something much narrower:

> Under the household-attribution framework used here, the present value of mandatory pension benefits received later in life exceeds the present value of mandatory pension contributions paid during working life.

This is exactly why LCER cannot be built by mechanically adding tax rates and contribution rates.

If benefits come back, they have to be deducted.

Otherwise this is not measurement.

It is just addition.

------

## 4. Why does Canadian public healthcare not show up as a huge negative extraction?

There is another place where it is very easy to make a mistake:

**Canadian healthcare.**

Because Canada has public healthcare, it is tempting to say:

if the government will spend large amounts on this household’s healthcare in the future, shouldn’t all of that be treated as a benefit and deducted from LCER?

No.

The reason is simple.

Canadian public healthcare is financed mainly through:

**general taxation.**

That means the personal income tax, GST/HST/PST already counted above jointly finance healthcare, education, roads, policing, and other public services.

If I first count general taxes and then construct a separate “health contribution,” I double count the cost.

But the reverse problem is just as serious.

If I deduct total public healthcare spending as a household benefit, while refusing to value public education, roads, policing, and other public services on the same basis, I create a one-sided benefit adjustment:

**healthcare is credited back, while other public services are ignored.**

So in the Canadian model:

> **Health extraction is recorded separately as 0.**

The Ontario Health Premium is already included in personal income tax.

This does not mean Canadian healthcare has no value.

Nor does it mean public healthcare has no cost.

It only means:

**inside the LCER household balance sheet, there is no clean standalone healthcare cash flow that can be separated from general taxation without creating double counting.**

At this point, the conventional part of the Canadian system is already clear:

- Toronto: **19.6%**
- Winnipeg: **20.6%**

If the article ended here, we would actually arrive at a counterintuitive conclusion:

> **Winnipeg has the higher lifetime fiscal and social-insurance burden.**

So why does Toronto end up at 34.1%?

The real difference begins now.

## 5. Housing is what actually separates Toronto from Winnipeg

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/toronto-ai-city-view.jpg" alt="toronto-ai-city-view" /></figure>

In 2021, the representative home price used here for Toronto is:

**CAD 900,000.**

Winnipeg:

**CAD 340,000.**

Relative to one year of household cash wages:

Toronto:

> **6.47×**

Winnipeg:

> **2.93×**

That is already a large difference.

But the same rule from the previous articles still applies:

**an expensive house does not mean the entire house price is institutional extraction.**

A CAD 900,000 home does not contain CAD 900,000 of institutional cost.

There is a building.

There is land.

There is location.

There is ordinary market scarcity.

And when the household pays CAD 900,000, it also receives an asset worth CAD 900,000.

The real question is:

> **Without specific housing-supply restrictions, would this house still be this expensive?**

That is where the Canadian case becomes interesting.

The C.D. Howe Institute compared market prices of newly built detached homes across major Canadian metropolitan areas with their minimum profitable physical production cost.

Its long-run average estimate suggests that the **supply-barrier gap** is approximately:

- Toronto: **32% of final price**
- Winnipeg: **12%**

That gap may reflect development charges, restricted land supply, infrastructure constraints, transportation bottlenecks, and limited development competition.

It is not a tax bill issued directly by the government.

But together, these constraints create one result:

> **Housing supply cannot fully respond to demand, so scarcity is capitalized into home prices.**

I do not directly use the study’s absolute dollar amounts.

The study focuses mainly on new detached homes, while this model needs a representative citywide home value.

So I use proportional mapping:

- Toronto: **900,000 × 32% = CAD 288,000**
- Winnipeg: **340,000 × 12% = CAD 40,800**

This needs to be stated clearly:

it is an:

**inferred estimate.**

It does not mean that the government “takes CAD 288,000” from every CAD 900,000 Toronto home.

And it does not mean that without planning rules the house would automatically be exactly CAD 288,000 cheaper.

It means:

> **Based on available research on housing-supply barriers, this is the central proxy used here for the identifiable institutional component of constrained housing supply.**

This is also the largest source of uncertainty in the Canadian model.

So the confidence level is:

**Medium.**

But the exact precision of CAD 288,000 is not the most important point.

The mechanism is.

## 6. What Toronto is really selling may not be housing

People often explain Toronto housing prices with a perfectly reasonable sentence:

> Everyone wants to live in Toronto.

Of course.

A city with more jobs, higher wages, deeper industries, and denser social networks should naturally attract more housing demand.

But hidden inside that explanation is an assumption:

**when demand rises, supply has to stand still.**

That is the real issue.

Rising demand can produce two very different outcomes.

One path is:

> More people want to enter  
> → home prices rise  
> → development increases  
> → density increases  
> → new housing enters the market  
> → scarcity is gradually absorbed by supply.

The other path is:

> More people want to enter  
> → home prices rise  
> → supply fails to expand at the same pace  
> → access to the city becomes increasingly scarce  
> → urban productivity and job opportunities are capitalized into residential property values.

Toronto is closer to the second path.

At that point, a household is no longer buying only:

80 square meters, 100 square meters, or a detached house.

It is also buying:

**the right to enter the city’s labor market.**

Why can a household acquire a representative home in Winnipeg for CAD 340,000, while Toronto requires CAD 900,000?

It is obviously not because Toronto’s bricks cost more than twice as much.

What is really expensive is:

**location.**

And where does the value of location come from?

From jobs.

From firms.

From industry clusters.

From schools.

From infrastructure.

From social networks.

From all the productivity advantages the city has accumulated over time.

This creates a very interesting transfer of wealth.

The city first creates opportunity.

Opportunity attracts people.

People increase housing demand.

Housing supply fails to expand at the same pace.

And then:

> **Value that originally came from urban productivity is capitalized into housing.**

A worker moving to Toronto may indeed earn a higher wage.

But before that higher wage fully arrives in his pocket, the housing market is already waiting for him.

That is why I do not think “Toronto housing is expensive” is a precise enough description.

What is really happening is:

> **A successful city is turning its own success into an increasingly expensive entry ticket.**

------

## 7. And this entry ticket is not paid only once

High housing prices create a second layer of cost.

They determine how much money the household must borrow.

Under the standard assumption used throughout this series:

> 30% down payment, 70% mortgage.

Toronto:

home price: CAD 900,000.

Mortgage:

> **CAD 630,000.**

Winnipeg:

home price: CAD 340,000.

Mortgage:

> **CAD 238,000.**

At this point, another important difference appears between Canada and the United States.

In the U.S. article, the model used a typical 30-year fixed-rate mortgage.

That allows a household to lock in the rate for a long period at the time of purchase.

Canada works differently.

Canadian mortgages typically use shorter terms and are repeatedly renewed.

In 2021, Canada was still in an unusually low-rate environment.

Bank of Canada and CMHC data show that five-year fixed mortgage rates were roughly around 2%.

If I assume that because the home was purchased in 2021, the household can finance the next 25 years permanently at around 2%, I would be turning a temporary monetary environment into:

**a 25-year institutional condition.**

So the central model uses:

first five years:

**approximately 2.18%.**

Following twenty years:

**4.0% long-run normalized rate.**

The 4% is not a forecast.

It simply refuses to make another obviously unrealistic assumption:

**that pandemic-era ultra-low rates should be projected forever.**

The resulting present value of mortgage interest is approximately:

Toronto:

> **CAD 203,853**

Equal to:

> **4.9% of lifetime labor value**

Winnipeg:

> **CAD 77,011**

Equal to:

> **2.2%**

Housing institutions now create a second transmission channel:

> **Supply constraint  
> → higher home price  
> → larger mortgage principal  
> → more interest paid over the following decades.**

The mortgage is not the first problem.

The bank did not invent a CAD 630,000 borrowing need out of nowhere.

It is simply continuing to collect on the housing-price structure that came before it.

------

## 8. Even after the house is purchased, the entry ticket is still not fully paid

Then there is:

**property tax.**

Annual property tax on the representative home is approximately:

- Toronto: **CAD 5,080**
- Winnipeg: **CAD 3,162**

Under the standard household model, the family buys at age 35 and holds the property until roughly age 85:

**50 years.**

Discounted over that period, property tax alone contributes approximately:

- Toronto: **3.2 percentage points of LCER**
- Winnipeg: **2.4 percentage points**

There is something important here.

Winnipeg’s home price is only about 38% of Toronto’s.

But its lifetime property-tax burden does not fall proportionally.

So housing institutional cost cannot be understood only through:

**purchase price.**

It appears at least in two separate places.

One occurs when the household:

**enters housing.**

The other occurs when the household:

**holds housing over time.**

Toronto also imposes both provincial and municipal land-transfer taxes.

Even after first-time buyer rebates, the one-time land-transfer tax on a CAD 900,000 home is approximately:

**CAD 20,475.**

Winnipeg:

**CAD 4,450.**

Once the following are placed on the same lifetime cash-flow sheet:

- supply-barrier proxy;
- land-transfer tax;
- recurring property tax;

the present value of housing institutional cost becomes:

Toronto:

> **CAD 386,630**

Equal to:

> **9.3% of lifetime labor value**

Winnipeg:

> **CAD 120,263**

Equal to:

> **3.4%**

Then add mortgage financing:

Toronto housing institutions + housing finance:

> **14.2%**

Winnipeg:

> **5.6%**

Now we finally see where the real difference between the two cities comes from.

## 9. How can one Canada produce two completely different Canadas?

Now look at the numbers again:

| Item | Toronto | Winnipeg |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net fiscal / social extraction | **19.6%** | **20.6%** |
| Housing institutional cost | **9.3%** | **3.4%** |
| Mortgage interest | **4.9%** | **2.2%** |
| Excess education competition | **0.3%** | **0.4%** |
| **LCER** | **34.1%** | **26.7%** |

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/winnipeg-ai-city-view.jpg" alt="winnipeg-ai-city-view" /></figure>

At this point, the structure is clear.

If we only look at taxes and social insurance:

Winnipeg is heavier.

What pushes Toronto to 34.1% is housing.

And not simply:

“Toronto houses are expensive.”

The full chain is:

> **The city creates more opportunities  
> → more workers need to enter  
> → housing demand rises  
> → supply fails to respond at the same pace  
> → scarcity is capitalized into home prices  
> → households take on larger mortgage principal  
> → mortgages extend high prices into decades of financing cost  
> → property tax continues charging during long-term ownership.**

That is what makes the 7.4 percentage-point gap between Toronto and Winnipeg so interesting.

The same country.

The same CPP.

The same public healthcare system.

Broadly the same federal tax structure.

Yet very different outcomes.

So Canada’s high cost of living, at least in this model, is not simply:

**“A welfare state with high taxes.”**

There is another major mechanism:

> **how urban institutions allocate access to high-productivity regions.**

## 10. Education is small, and I did not count all the other things people say are “expensive in Canada”

Education is not a major driver here.

In 2021, average Canadian household spending on goods and services was around CAD 67,126, of which approximately CAD 1,996 was categorized as education and reading materials.

That is:

**2.97%.**

Using the same 1.5% normal private-education benchmark as in the rest of the series, and counting only the years in which child-related education spending actually occurs, the lifetime present value is approximately:

**CAD 13,699.**

That corresponds to:

- Toronto: **0.3%**
- Winnipeg: **0.4%**

So education is not an important source of Canadian LCER.

There are also many things Canadians often complain about:

- telecom;
- airlines;
- banking;
- dairy;
- supply management;
- market concentration;
- industry access barriers.

These issues are real subjects for analysis.

But:

“Canadian telecom is expensive”

and:

“Canadian telecom institutions cause an ordinary household to lose X amount of lifetime labor value”

are not the same statement.

Without a reliable household-lifecycle counterfactual, I cannot simply add a few percentage points because an industry “feels” institutionally expensive.

So the model still records:

**Monopoly / Other: Not Included.**

That does not mean:

**0 = does not exist.**

It means:

> **Existence does not equal measurability.**

A model that only gives itself permission to add costs, but never permission to exclude them, eventually stops being a model.

It becomes decoration for an opinion.

## 11. Add it all up: Toronto 34.1%, Winnipeg 26.7%

The Canadian central model is:

| Item | Toronto | Winnipeg |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net fiscal / social extraction | 19.6% | 20.6% |
| Housing institutional cost | 9.3% | 3.4% |
| Mortgage interest | 4.9% | 2.2% |
| Excess education competition | 0.3% | 0.4% |
| Monopoly / Other | Not Included | Not Included |
| **Core LCER** | **34.1%** | **26.7%** |
| **Total LCER** | **34.1%** | **26.7%** |

So:

Toronto:

**19.6 + 9.3 + 4.9 + 0.3 = 34.1%.**

Winnipeg:

**20.6 + 3.4 + 2.2 + 0.4 = 26.7%.**

In money terms:

- A Toronto household creates approximately **CAD 4.178 million** in lifetime labor value.

  The institutional lifetime net costs currently captured by the model are approximately **CAD 1.42 million**.

- A Winnipeg household creates approximately **CAD 3.503 million**.

  Corresponding institutional lifetime cost is approximately **CAD 0.94 million**.

If a simple Canadian summary number is needed for the cross-country database, the equal-weight average of the two cities is:

> **30.4%.**

But this needs to be stated carefully.

It is not a population-weighted “national Canadian LCER.”

The formal Canadian dataset should still retain two rows:

> **Toronto: 34.1%.**
>
> **Winnipeg: 26.7%.**

Because the difference between those two numbers is precisely what makes Canada interesting.

## 12. Put China, the United States, and Canada together, and the question becomes clearer

The series now has three countries.

- China
  - Shanghai: **about 102%**
  - Nanjing: **75.3%**
- United States
  - New York: **30.3%**
  - Chicago: **23.0%**
- Canada
  - Toronto: **34.1%**
  - Winnipeg: **26.7%**

If all I do is rank those numbers from highest to lowest, I think the entire point of the model is lost.

What matters is:

**how each number is formed.**

China showed me that:

> **Low income-tax rates do not mean low institutional costs for households.**

Land can charge.

Housing can charge.

Long-term mortgage leverage can turn today’s institutional cost into thirty years of future cash flow.

The United States then showed something else:

> **High visible taxation does not automatically produce a higher final LCER.**

New York’s conventional fiscal and social-insurance burden is even higher than Shanghai’s.

But because housing has not been pushed to the same extreme relative to ordinary household income, and because long-term financing does not magnify that cost by the same order of magnitude, the final outcome is very different.

Canada introduces a third case.

Toronto and Winnipeg do not even require an international comparison.

Inside one country, we can already see:

> **A broadly similar fiscal system combined with a very different housing structure can produce a completely different lifetime cost profile for ordinary households.**

Canada therefore pushes me toward a more precise understanding of what LCER is actually measuring.

It may never have been:

**How much does the state take?**

A better question is:

> **Where does a society place the institutional costs that ordinary households cannot easily avoid?**

Some are placed on the paycheck.

Some are placed on consumption.

Some enter pensions first and are partly returned after retirement.

Some are placed in land.

Some are placed in housing supply.

Some are then carried forward through mortgages, turning asset prices into decades of future cash flow.

What makes Canada particularly interesting is this:

**a substantial share of the cost is placed on the act of entering the city.**

## 13. What is really expensive in Canada may not be taxes

Suppose a young person stays in Winnipeg.

He gets a city with more ordinary income, but housing that remains relatively accessible.

Suppose he moves to Toronto.

He may get higher wages.

More jobs.

Better industries.

A deeper labor market.

More firms.

More social networks.

From an individual point of view, moving to Toronto can be perfectly rational.

The problem is:

**everyone else is making the same rational choice.**

So high-productivity cities keep attracting people.

If housing supply expands with demand, then a meaningful share of the productivity gains generated by the city can remain with the new entrants.

But if housing supply is persistently constrained, the outcome changes.

Wages rise.

Demand rises.

Housing prices rise even faster.

Part of the gain a worker expected to capture by entering a higher-productivity city begins to be absorbed by housing assets.

Higher housing prices then create larger mortgages.

Larger mortgages create more interest.

Long-term ownership adds recurring property tax.

In the end, a young person may think he is doing one thing:

> **moving to Toronto for a better job.**

From the perspective of a lifetime household balance sheet, he is doing something else at the same time:

> **buying the right to enter Toronto.**

That may be the most important difference between Canada and the first two countries in this series.

The China article ended with one question:

How much of the wealth a household creates over a lifetime can actually remain in the household sector?

The U.S. article moved the question one step further:

Where, exactly, does a society charge ordinary households?

Canada allows the question to move one step further again:

> **When a city creates more opportunity, who ultimately captures the value created by that opportunity?**

If wages rise by 10%,

but the price of gaining access to those jobs rises even more through housing;

if the productivity gains associated with entering the city are increasingly capitalized into land and property;

then “urban prosperity” begins to produce a strange result for new entrants:

**There really are more opportunities.**

**But the price of buying access to those opportunities also keeps rising.**

At that point, the question is no longer simply:

Why is Toronto housing so expensive?

The real question becomes:

> **If a society concentrates its best opportunities in a small number of cities, while keeping housing access to those cities persistently scarce, are those cities raising ordinary people’s productivity—or selling admission to productivity?**

What is expensive in Canada may never have been just taxes.

It may be:

**the right to enter a successful city.**
