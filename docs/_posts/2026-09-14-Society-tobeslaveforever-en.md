Suppose someone told you this:

> For an ordinary dual-income Shanghai household employed in the private sector, the amount extracted over its lifetime through taxes and fees, social insurance, land finance, housing finance, institutionally induced education competition, upstream monopoly, and related channels can, under the model used in this article, amount to roughly **100%** of the labor value the household creates over its working life.

The first reaction is likely to be:

**How can that possibly be true?**

The numbers we normally see look nothing like this.

China's personal income tax is not especially high. For a large share of ordinary salaried households, personal income tax is not even one of the most visible items in the monthly budget. And if we look at the tax-and-social-insurance measures commonly used by international institutions, China's explicit fiscal burden is nowhere near 70% or 80%, let alone 100%.

So either this 100% figure is so absurd that it is not worth discussing, or the conventional idea of the “tax burden” has been missing, from the beginning, some of the largest channels through which wealth is transferred out of ordinary Chinese households.

I believe the latter is closer to the truth.

For an ordinary Chinese household, the largest burdens do not occur only on the payslip.

They also appear in land, in housing prices, in the 30-year mortgage required to pay those prices, in education competition, and in the costs passed down through upstream sectors such as energy, finance, electricity, and telecommunications until they eventually reach the prices households pay for goods and services.

So this article is not calculating a conventional “tax rate.”

I use a different term:

**the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate.**

By “extraction,” I mean the share of a household's lifetime labor value that is transferred out of the household sector either directly by the government, or indirectly through systems established, controlled, or maintained by the government, including compulsory contributions, land finance, the housing-finance mechanism, institutionally induced competition, and upstream monopoly structures. Where pensions, health insurance, or other benefits can reasonably be attributed back to the household, those benefits are deducted from the corresponding contributions.

Taxes, land-transfer revenue, mortgage interest, education-competition costs, and monopoly-related welfare losses are obviously not the same accounting item.

But they share one defining feature:

**They are not simply voluntary market expenditures made by a household in exchange for ordinary goods or services. They arise from specific institutional structures, and the resulting costs ultimately fall on the household sector.**

That is what this article attempts to measure.

So let us take one household and calculate the items one by one.

## 1. Start with the denominator: how much labor value does this household create over a lifetime?

We use Shanghai in 2021 as the baseline.

The reason is straightforward. A large number of urban households that still carry heavy mortgages today entered the housing market during the high-price period around 2017–2021. Using housing prices after the subsequent correction to estimate what those households actually faced at the time would simply erase historical costs that were already incurred.

In 2021, the average annual wage of employees in Shanghai's urban private-sector establishments was:

**RMB 96,011 per person.**

If both spouses work in ordinary private-sector jobs, the household's annual cash wage is therefore:

**96,011 × 2 = RMB 192,022.**

But RMB 192,022 is not the full cost borne by employers for these two workers.

Around 2021, Shanghai employers also paid social-insurance contributions of roughly 16% for pensions, about 10.5% for medical and maternity insurance combined, around 0.5% for unemployment insurance, plus a minimum-tier work-injury contribution. The employer-side total was approximately:

**27.16%.**

The annual labor cost associated with these two positions was therefore about:

**192,022 × (1 + 27.16%) ≈ RMB 244,175.**

This article treats that RMB 244,175 as the household's annual labor value.

The next question is the important one:

**What should RMB 244,175 per year become over a 35-year working life?**

A very common mistake is to look at the historical rise in China's urban average wages and then assume that an ordinary household's real income will keep growing by 1% or 2% every year for the next 35 years, or even to extrapolate past average-wage growth directly into the future.

I do not do that.

The model assumes:

**Long-run real wage growth = 0%.**

And 0% is not a pessimistic assumption.

On the contrary, for an ordinary private-sector worker in China today, I regard it as a fairly conservative assumption.

China's growth rate is already well below that of the high-growth decades, and employment and wages in many industries are under pressure. More importantly, the lifetime earnings path of an ordinary worker is not the same thing as the average wage growth rate reported for currently employed urban workers.

There is a statistical issue here that is easy to miss and potentially very misleading.

Suppose 100 people were employed last year and together earned RMB 10 million.

The average wage was:

**RMB 10 million ÷ 100 = RMB 100,000.**

Now suppose the economy weakens and 50 of them lose their jobs this year.

The remaining 50 workers earn a combined RMB 6 million.

If the statistic covers only people who are still employed:

**RMB 6 million ÷ 50 = RMB 120,000.**

The published number would therefore tell us:

> **Average wages rose by 20%.**

But the original group of 100 people earned only RMB 6 million in total this year.

If the 50 unemployed people remain in the denominator of the original cohort:

**RMB 6 million ÷ 100 = RMB 60,000.**

That is not a 20% increase.

It is a decline from RMB 100,000 to RMB 60,000:

**a real drop of 40%.**

There is no arithmetic contradiction here.

The denominator changed.

Once a person becomes unemployed, that person is no longer part of the population of “employed persons,” and therefore disappears from the denominator of the average-wage statistic as well.

That does not make the statistic itself fraudulent. It answers a specific question: how much do people who remain employed earn on average?

The problem comes when a cross-sectional average for currently employed workers is used to project the 35-year longitudinal income path of the same ordinary workers.

That can create a serious bias.

A 40-year-old who loses his job does not automatically receive a 5% pay increase merely because the next year's private-sector average wage rises by 5%.

A worker whose monthly salary falls from RMB 20,000 to RMB 8,000 may, statistically, move into another firm, another industry, or another sample altogether.

And for ordinary private-sector workers in China, age discrimination, occupational obsolescence, industry decline, corporate failure, long-term pay cuts, and difficulty re-entering formal employment after age 35 or 40 are real lifecycle risks that cannot simply be ignored.

A realistic 35-year earnings path may therefore look something like this: income rises when workers are young, peaks in middle age, then stagnates; for some people it declines, while others leave their previous employment track entirely in the latter half of their careers.

Under these conditions, assuming that a household's **real purchasing-power income neither rises nor falls over 35 years** is already a relatively generous estimate of the long-run earning capacity of an ordinary private-sector household.

So the model uses:

**Long-run real wage growth = 0%.**

It then applies a 2% real discount rate.

The present-value annuity factor for 35 years is about 25.

Therefore:

**244,175 × 25 ≈ RMB 6.10 million.**

That RMB 6.10 million is the denominator of the Shanghai model.

> Over 35 years of work, measured in real value at the beginning of the working life, this couple creates roughly RMB 6.10 million in labor value.

Every extraction item below is measured against that same RMB 6.10 million denominator.

## 2. The first layer of extraction: how should taxes and social insurance actually be counted?

Start with the most familiar component: social insurance.

There are two opposite mistakes that are easy to make here.

The first is to say:

**Whatever the government collects in social-insurance contributions should be counted in full as a household loss.**

That is clearly unreasonable.

Pensions are paid later. Health insurance covers part of medical expenditure. Unemployment, work-injury, and maternity insurance also provide real benefits.

The opposite error is to say:

**All social-insurance contributions are eventually returned as benefits, so every yuan paid in is economically equivalent to a yuan received back.**

That is equally difficult to defend.

The principle used in this article is simple:

> **The model does not treat “how much the government collected” as the loss. It calculates: contributions minus the effective benefits that can actually be attributed back to the household.**

Start with pensions.

Independent research has long placed the effective pension replacement rate for urban enterprise employees in the low-40% range, with public-sector replacement rates substantially higher. World Inequality Lab data likewise show the replacement rate for formal urban workers falling from roughly 70% in earlier periods to around 44% in more recent years.

For an ordinary formal employee outside the public sector, this article therefore assumes:

**Retirement-income replacement rate: 45%.**

Assume:

35 years of work;

20 years of retirement;

and a 2% real discount rate.

Under these assumptions, the lifecycle present value of pension contributions is equivalent to approximately:

**19.0% of household labor cost.**

The present value of the pension income eventually received is only about:

**11.7% of household labor cost.**

The lifecycle net gap for pensions is therefore:

**19.0% − 11.7% = 7.3 percentage points.**

In other words:

> **On a lifecycle present-value basis, the household pays in the equivalent of 19.0, receives back about 11.7, and is left with a net gap of roughly 7.3 percentage points.**

Now consider medical insurance.

Employer and employee medical-insurance contributions together are roughly 11% of cash wages. Converted into the labor-cost denominator used in this article, their lifecycle present value is equivalent to approximately:

**8.7%.**

Under a very simple government-accounting approach, it would be easy to say that if 8.7% is collected in medical-insurance contributions, then 8.7% has also been converted into medical benefits.

I do not use that assumption.

Being enrolled in medical insurance and receiving benefits equal in value to the contributions are not the same thing.

CFPS household-survey evidence and research on catastrophic health expenditure show that Chinese households continue to face substantial out-of-pocket medical risk even after enrolling in basic health insurance.

World Bank household-level modeling likewise finds high out-of-pocket medical expenditure in China and explicitly notes that such burdens can deter households from using medically necessary care.

This means that looking only at how much the insurance fund spends does not tell us how much effective benefit an ordinary household actually receives.

Some medical needs are not reimbursed.

Some costs remain out of pocket.

Some medicines and treatments fall outside reimbursement catalogues.

Some households delay or forgo necessary care because they cannot afford the unreimbursed share.

So the neutral case in this model does not assume that:

**100% of the value of medical-insurance contributions is converted into effective household medical benefits.**

Instead, I assume:

**Effective benefit-conversion rate = 75%.**

Thus:

Lifecycle present value of medical-insurance contributions:

**8.7%.**

Effective medical benefits attributable to the household:

**8.7% × 75% ≈ 6.5%.**

Net gap:

**8.7% − 6.5% ≈ 2.2%.**

If we instead used a more pessimistic, but still not extreme, effective-return rate of 60%, the net medical gap would rise to:

**about 3.5 percentage points.**

This article does not use that higher figure.

It keeps the 75% neutral assumption.

There is also one category that I deliberately do not quantify:

**special medical treatment for senior officials and other privileged groups.**

China has long had institutional and historical evidence of special medical services and unequal treatment across status groups.

But I do not currently have a sufficiently reliable national total from which to calculate the additional loss imposed on ordinary households by that allocation of medical resources.

So I leave it out.

That means that, at least in the medical-insurance component, the current model is more likely to **understate the loss borne by ordinary households from privileged allocation of medical resources than to overstate it.**

Pensions and medical insurance alone therefore produce a lifecycle net gap of:

**7.3% + 2.2% = 9.5%.**

We then add the net gap from other social-insurance programs, personal income tax, and the value-added tax, consumption tax, and other taxes embedded in ordinary consumption.

The model's central estimate for the combined lifecycle net burden of social insurance and taxes is therefore approximately:

**14%.**

That is:

**RMB 6.10 million × 14% ≈ RMB 850,000.**

One point needs to be emphasized again:

> **The 14% figure is not the amount the government collected. It is the lifecycle net burden after deducting pensions, health-insurance benefits, and other effective benefits that can reasonably be attributed back to the household.**

At this point, the result still does not look extraordinary.

A household creates RMB 6.10 million in lifetime labor value, and the net burden from explicit taxes and social insurance is about RMB 850,000.

The factor that changes the scale of the model is the next one:

**land.**

## 3. What transforms the model is not personal income tax, but land finance

In 2021, the transaction value of Shanghai's commodity housing market was approximately:

**RMB 584.779 billion.**

The number of transactions was about:

**92,000 units.**

That implies an average transaction price per home of roughly:

**RMB 584.779 billion ÷ 92,000 ≈ RMB 6.356 million.**

Now place that figure next to the private-sector dual-income household described above.

Annual pre-tax household wage income:

**RMB 192,000.**

Average commodity-housing price:

**RMB 6.356 million.**

Therefore:

**6.356 million ÷ 192,000 ≈ 33.1.**

In other words:

> **An average Shanghai commodity home in 2021 cost the equivalent of roughly 33 years of the combined pre-tax wages of two ordinary private-sector workers.**

Not 33 years of one person's pay.

Thirty-three years of both spouses' pay combined.

And that is before deducting taxes, social insurance, food, clothing, healthcare, education, transportation, or retirement saving.

It is simply the mechanical sum of all pre-tax wages.

So the real question is no longer:

“Are Shanghai housing prices high?”

It is:

> **How can a basic household asset in a city be priced at a level that is almost equal to the combined cash wages of an ordinary couple over an entire working life?**

To answer that, RMB 6.356 million cannot be treated as one indivisible market price.

We have to look inside it.

## 4. The first institutional extraction inside housing prices: land and the real-estate fiscal chain

The entire housing price obviously cannot be counted as extraction.

A home contains real construction value: steel, cement, glass, equipment, wages for construction workers, design, construction management, development management, and developer profit.

The household also ends up owning a real housing asset.

So this article never counts the entire RMB 6.356 million housing price as extraction.

The relevant question is narrower:

**How much of that price corresponds to land and the fiscal revenues built around real estate?**

The *2024 China Land Finance Report* estimated the composition of housing prices across a sample of cities including Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Shenzhen.

In 2023, land cost represented about 38% of housing prices across the sample; in first-tier cities, the estimate was approximately:

**41.7%.**

Real-estate-related taxes and fees averaged about:

**17.2%.**

For first-tier cities, the estimate was approximately:

**17.3%.**

Combined, the first-tier-city figure was therefore around:

**59%.**

To allow for differences in year, project mix, and methodology, this article does not simply use 59% as an absolute figure.

The Shanghai model uses a slightly lower central parameter:

**57.5%.**

Therefore:

**RMB 6.356 million × 57.5% ≈ RMB 3.655 million.**

That means that, in the model, about:

**RMB 3.655 million**

of an average RMB 6.356 million Shanghai commodity home corresponds to land and the real-estate fiscal chain.

At this point, a common objection has to be addressed.

People often say:

> But the house still belongs to me. How can you count the housing price as money that was taken away?

Of course you cannot.

That is precisely why this article does not do so.

The physical structure of the home remains a household asset, so the housing principal is not counted in full as extraction.

Land transfer and the real-estate fiscal chain are a different matter.

The developer first acquires land-use rights from the government.

The land payment becomes part of development cost.

That cost is then embedded in the housing price.

The household ultimately pays it when purchasing the home.

Once that transfer of wealth has taken place, it does not disappear merely because the household now owns the property.

If A later sells the home to B, and B later sells it to C, that is an exchange of assets within the household sector. The government does not return the original land-transfer revenue simply because the home has entered the secondary market.

So:

**“The household owns a housing asset”**

and

**“The wealth previously paid into the land-finance system still belongs to the household”**

are two entirely different propositions.

## 5. Land and the real-estate fiscal chain alone already reach 49.1%

To avoid directly adding future expenditure to labor value measured at the beginning of the working life, the model assumes the household buys its home in the tenth year of its career.

The real discount rate remains 2%.

The RMB 3.655 million land-and-real-estate fiscal component therefore has a present value at the start of the working life of:

**3.655 million ÷ 1.02¹⁰ ≈ RMB 2.998 million.**

Divide that by lifetime labor value of RMB 6.104 million:

**2.998 million ÷ 6.104 million ≈ 49.1%.**

In other words:

> **The land and real-estate fiscal chain embedded in a single average Shanghai commodity home is equivalent to about 49% of the lifetime labor value created by this ordinary dual-income household.**

Now combine that with the 14% net tax-and-social-insurance burden:

Taxes and net social-insurance burden:

**14.0%.**

Land and real-estate fiscal chain:

**49.1%.**

The total is already:

**63.1%.**

And we have not yet counted the mortgage.

## 6. Land finance does not end on the day the home is purchased; it creates a second layer of cost through housing finance

A household earning only RMB 192,000 in pre-tax wages per year obviously cannot pay RMB 6.356 million in cash.

So the housing system requires a second mechanism:

**debt.**

Assume a 30% down payment.

The mortgage principal is therefore approximately:

**RMB 6.356 million × 70% ≈ RMB 4.449 million.**

Around 2021, mortgage rates for first-home buyers in Shanghai were at times close to 5%.

But assuming that a 5% rate remained unchanged for 30 years would clearly overstate actual financing costs.

A large share of Chinese mortgages are priced off the LPR framework. LPRs were subsequently reduced, and rates on outstanding mortgages also went through concentrated downward adjustments.

So this model uses a declining interest-rate path.

Approximately:

2021: 5.0%;

2022: still close to 5.0%;

2023: around 4.6%;

2024: around 3.9%;

long-run central rate thereafter: around 3.2%.

Each time the interest rate changes, the payment is recalculated using the remaining principal and remaining term.

Under this treatment, the total nominal interest paid over 30 years on a RMB 4.449 million mortgage is approximately:

**RMB 2.766 million.**

But RMB 2.766 million cannot simply be dropped into the lifecycle model either.

The interest is paid gradually over the next 30 years.

So it must be discounted according to when each payment actually occurs, while also recognizing that the mortgage begins only in the tenth year of the household's working life.

The resulting present value of mortgage interest is approximately:

**RMB 1.881 million.**

Divide that by lifetime labor value of RMB 6.104 million:

**1.881 million ÷ 6.104 million ≈ 30.8%.**

Why is this 30.8% included in the “comprehensive extraction” measure?

Because this article is not measuring government fiscal revenue alone.

Mortgage interest is obviously not itself fiscal revenue.

The mechanism is this:

**Land finance first raises the institutional component of housing prices to a level that ordinary households cannot pay out of current income. Households are therefore forced to use decades of financial leverage, and financing that institutional price generates a second layer of cost.**

The transmission chain is:

> **Land regime → high land cost enters housing prices → housing prices exceed ordinary households' current payment capacity → households must assume long-term debt → households pay decades of financing cost.**

Mortgage interest in this model is therefore not treated as an isolated market price.

It is the second-stage cost created when the first institutional cost is transmitted through housing finance into the household's lifetime income stream.

## 7. With only three components, the total already reaches 93.9%

At this point, we have counted only three broad categories:

| Item | Share of lifetime labor value |
| --- | ---: |
| Net burden of social insurance, personal income tax, and consumption taxes | **14.0%** |
| Land and real-estate fiscal chain | **49.1%** |
| Total mortgage interest | **30.8%** |
| **Total** | **93.9%** |

In other words:

> **Once we count only explicit taxes and the net social-insurance gap, the land and real-estate fiscal chain, and the financing cost created by housing leverage, roughly 94% of the lifetime labor value of an ordinary Shanghai dual-income household has already entered these systems.**

We still have not counted education competition.

We have not counted the upstream monopoly structure in energy, finance, electricity, and telecommunications.

And we have not counted the additional tax burden associated with typical gasoline-powered transportation.

So the real question is no longer:

“Is 100% too high?”

It becomes:

> **Is the remaining 6.1% enough to cover the institutional costs that have not yet entered the model?**

## 8. Where Does the 1.7% Education Cost Come From?

Education is one of the easiest components of this model to miscalculate.

Chinese households spend heavily on their children’s education, but that does not mean all education spending should be classified as institutional extraction. Ordinary tuition, books, school supplies, and voluntarily chosen educational consumption are normal costs of raising a child.

What this model attempts to measure is narrower:

> **How much additional private education spending do Chinese households bear above a reasonable baseline because of the intensity of educational competition?**

Start with the Chinese data.

Research based on CFPS household microdata finds that Chinese households with school-age children spend, on average:

**17.1% of household income**

on education.

Measured against total household expenditure, education accounts for approximately:

**7.9%.**

That 7.9% cannot simply be added to the lifecycle extraction rate. Families in every country incur some education costs when they have children in school.

We therefore need a benchmark.

This model uses Japan.

Stanford’s international comparison of the relevant research places Japanese household education spending at approximately:

**1%–2% of total household expenditure.**

The excess private education spending borne by Chinese households is therefore approximately:

**7.9% − 1%–2% = 5.9%–6.9% of total household expenditure.**

Using the midpoint gives:

**approximately 6.4%.**

In other words, during the years in which a child is in education, for every 100 yuan of household expenditure, roughly 6.4 yuan represents education spending above the Japanese benchmark.

Japan is also not an especially lenient benchmark.

It is not a society without private tutoring or intense educational competition. OECD data indicate that at the lower-secondary level alone, Japanese households may spend the equivalent of approximately:

**4.6%–6.5% of average annual earnings per child**

on juku, or private cram schools.

So the comparison is not between China and a country in which families face virtually no educational competition.

Quite the opposite:

> **Japan already has a mature tutoring culture and substantial competition for educational advancement. Yet Chinese households with school-age children still spend roughly 5.9%–6.9% more of total household expenditure on education.**

But that 6.4% still cannot be inserted directly into the lifecycle model.

The reason is important.

**The 6.4% figure represents excess household expenditure during the years when children are in education. The denominator of this article, however, is the total lifecycle labor value created by both spouses over 35 working years.**

A household does not bear school-age education costs in every one of those 35 years.

The calculation therefore requires one further conversion:

**Chinese household education expenditure: 7.9%
− Japanese household education expenditure: 1%–2%
= excess education expenditure during schooling years: 5.9%–6.9%
→ apply the excess only to the years in which the household actually bears child-education costs
→ discount those expenditures to the beginning of the household’s working lifecycle at a 2% real discount rate
→ divide the resulting present value by the couple’s total 35-year lifecycle labor cost.**

Once that adjustment is made, an annual excess education burden of roughly 5.9%–6.9% during the child-education period becomes approximately:

**1.6%–1.9% of total lifecycle labor value.**

This article uses the midpoint:

**1.7%.**

The distinction matters.

**6.4% is the estimated excess spending rate during the education years.**

**1.7% is the corresponding burden after those costs are discounted according to when they occur and then spread across the household’s entire 35-year lifecycle labor value.**

It would therefore be incorrect to say:

“Chinese households spend 6.4 percentage points more than Japanese households, so the lifecycle extraction rate rises by 6.4 percentage points.”

That would implicitly assume that the household pays this excess education cost every year throughout the couple’s entire 35-year working life.

The model does not make that assumption.

It includes only the lifecycle-adjusted amount:

**1.7 percentage points.**

Why should even this excess portion be treated as institutional in character?

Because educational competition is fundamentally competition over relative position.

When school selection, university admission, credential screening, and eventually access to desirable jobs depend heavily on where a child ranks relative to others, a household that cuts education spending is not merely choosing to buy fewer lessons.

It may also be accepting a deterioration in the child’s relative competitive position.

That creates a familiar escalation mechanism:

> **One household spends more to avoid falling behind
> → other households respond in the same way
> → aggregate education spending rises
> → the number of elite schools, selective university places, and high-quality jobs does not rise proportionally
> → the competitive baseline moves upward
> → spending that was once optional increasingly becomes necessary merely to preserve the same relative position.**

The model therefore does not classify all education consumption as extraction.

It includes only the **excess private education spending of Chinese households relative to the Japanese benchmark**, and then converts that excess into a lifecycle burden based on the years in which those costs are actually incurred.

The resulting estimate is:

**Additional education-system competition cost ≈ 1.7% of lifecycle labor value.**

Adding this to the 93.9% already accounted for in the Shanghai model gives:

**93.9% + 1.7% = 95.6%.**

## 9. Upstream monopoly: households do not need to pay a firm directly for the cost to reach them

The next component is less visible.

An ordinary consumer may never directly pay a large energy company something explicitly labeled a “monopoly tax.”

Nor does a household write a separate check to electricity, telecommunications, or large financial institutions for “institutional cost.”

But modern economies do not work by having consumers pay every upstream producer directly.

Costs travel down supply chains.

Oil enters transportation, chemicals, plastics, synthetic fibers, agricultural machinery, and packaging.

Electricity enters almost every form of industrial production.

Financing costs enter every business that depends on credit.

Telecommunications are embedded in nearly every part of modern commercial activity.

Suppose a restaurant simultaneously faces higher electricity costs, logistics costs, financing costs, and rent.

The owner will not forward the upstream firms' invoices to the customer unchanged.

Instead, the owner may raise the price of a bowl of noodles from RMB 25 to RMB 28, cut wages, accept a lower profit margin, or shut down altogether.

The cost ultimately returns to the household sector through prices, wages, and business exit.

So the right question is not:

**“Did the household pay this upstream firm directly?”**

It is:

> **After upstream market power propagates through the economy, how much household welfare is ultimately lost?**

A 2026 study published in the *Journal of Public Economics* used firm-level counterfactual simulations to estimate the effects of market power among upstream state-owned enterprises in China.

Using the economic structure of 2005 as its model baseline, the study found that removing the relevant upstream monopoly distortions could increase:

GDP by approximately:

**5.52%.**

and consumption-equivalent welfare by approximately:

**4.03%.**

This obviously does not mean that every Chinese household today bears an upstream-monopoly cost of exactly 4.03%.

Industrial structure, household consumption patterns, and the economy itself change over time.

So this article does not pretend that 4.03% is a precise tax rate.

It uses the study as a benchmark for the order of magnitude.

The Shanghai model adopts:

**4%.**

It also keeps a sensitivity range of:

**2%–8%.**

Therefore:

**95.6% + 4.0% = 99.6%.**

At this point, only 0.4 percentage points remain.

## 10. The final 0.6 percentage points come from a deliberately conservative gasoline-transport increment

For a typical household that owns a gasoline-powered car, the model adds only the incremental burden from fuel consumption tax and similar items not already captured in the ordinary consumption-tax component.

It does not add the full purchase price of the vehicle, total gasoline expenditure, expressway tolls, parking fees, insurance, or depreciation.

The reason is simple:

many of those items are ordinary purchases of goods and services, and some could overlap with costs already counted elsewhere.

So the model adds only:

**0.6 percentage points.**

The full Shanghai calculation is therefore:

| Item | Share of lifetime labor value |
| --- | ---: |
| Net burden of social insurance, personal income tax, and consumption taxes | **14.0%** |
| Land and real-estate fiscal chain | **49.1%** |
| Total mortgage interest | **30.8%** |
| Excess education-competition cost | **1.7%** |
| Upstream SOE monopoly/resource-misallocation cost | **4.0%** |
| Incremental cost of typical gasoline transportation | **0.6%** |
| **Total** | **100.2%** |

So under the model's central assumptions:

> **The Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate for the typical Shanghai household modeled here is about 100%.**

The direct sum is:

**100.2%.**

The 0.2 percentage point itself obviously has no statistical significance.

The model is not precise enough to justify a number to the nearest one-tenth of one percentage point.

What matters is this:

**the central estimate has crossed 100%.**

## 11. If the number exceeds 100%, why does the household not “go mathematically bankrupt”? 

At this point, an obvious question arises.

If the household creates only about RMB 6.10 million in lifetime labor value, yet the comprehensive extraction measure exceeds the equivalent of RMB 6.10 million, how can the household still buy a home, live, and retire?

This is not a mathematical paradox.

The balance sheet of a Chinese urban household has long ceased to be a one-generation model.

The answer is captured by a phrase that became widely used in China:

**“six wallets.”**

![littlefamily-01](https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/littlefamily-01.jpg)

A young couple buys the home.

Both sets of parents contribute to the down payment.

Some households even draw on grandparents' savings.

The young couple then takes responsibility for the next twenty or thirty years of mortgage payments.

Legally, the property may be registered to the young couple.

But economically, the purchase may be financed by three generations of accumulated wealth and future income.

So when the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate of a one-generation household exceeds 100%, it does not mean:

“the household violates an accounting identity.”

It means:

> **The lifetime labor value of this generation of ordinary workers is no longer sufficient, by itself, to close the household balance sheet.**

The gap must be filled by other sources of wealth.

Parents' savings.

Housing gains accumulated in the previous property cycle.

Pre-marital housing assets.

Demolition and relocation compensation.

Family gifts.

Inheritance.

In other words, the burden imposed on this generation has begun to draw on the stock of wealth accumulated by the previous generation.

That also helps explain why China can simultaneously display several features that may look contradictory:

high household saving;

high homeownership;

high household housing wealth;

high mortgage debt;

and low household consumption.

They are not necessarily contradictory.

Before buying a home, households save for the down payment.

After buying, they continue saving in order to service long-term debt.

The parents' lifetime savings are then concentrated into the next generation's home purchase.

The household may appear wealthy on paper because it owns a high-value property, while much of the family's accumulated wealth is simultaneously locked inside housing.

## 12. Not buying a home does not make land cost disappear from the economy

A common objection is:

> What if I simply do not buy a home?

For a specific household, that is of course possible.

A renter does not directly incur the down payment and mortgage on that particular home.

So the Shanghai 100% scenario in this article is explicitly defined as:

> **Two ordinary private-sector workers who buy an average Shanghai commodity home during the high-price period, finance 70% of the purchase with debt, work for 35 years, and raise one child.**

It is not an estimate for every Shanghai resident.

But once we move from the balance sheet of one household to the cost structure of the entire urban economy, not buying a home does not make land and housing costs disappear.

A landlord who buys a residential property still bears the land cost, asset price, and financing cost.

How much of that can be shifted to tenants depends on rental-market supply and demand. It would be wrong to assume mechanically that 100% can always be passed through.

But wherever pass-through is possible, some of the cost enters rent.

Move one step further downstream.

A restaurant rents commercial premises.

An education provider rents classrooms.

A convenience store rents a storefront.

A company rents office space.

Land and asset prices embedded in commercial property enter rent.

Rent then becomes a business cost.

The business has only a few ways to absorb it:

raise the price of goods and services;

compress employee wages;

accept a lower profit margin;

or exit the market.

Land cost can therefore travel through another chain:

> **Land price → real-estate asset price → commercial and residential rent → operating cost → prices of goods and services or worker wages → final household.**

This article obviously does not take the 49.1% already attributed to homeowners and then add the same amount again to renters.

That would be blatant double counting.

The point is narrower:

**Land finance does not affect only people who buy homes.**

It is an upstream institutional cost capable of propagating through the wider urban cost structure.

## 13. If Shanghai is too extreme, what happens in Nanjing?

Once the Shanghai figure reaches 100%, a very reasonable objection is:

> Shanghai's housing market is abnormal. Using Shanghai to say something about Chinese urban households more generally is not representative.

So let us leave Shanghai.

Not Beijing.

Not Shenzhen.

Consider a typical strong second-tier city:

**Nanjing.**

Nanjing has a relatively strong industrial base, more employment opportunities than most Chinese cities, household income well above the national average, and housing prices that remained elevated for much of the past decade.

It is not a super first-tier city.

That is precisely why Nanjing matters.

The point is not to prove that:

“Nanjing is the same as Shanghai.”

It is to test a different proposition:

> **Once we move away from an extreme housing market like Shanghai, does the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate quickly fall back toward the roughly 20% level implied by conventional tax-burden statistics?**

The answer is:

No.

## 14. The Nanjing model: both the denominator and housing price fall outside the first-tier cities

Around 2021, the average annual wage of employees in Nanjing's urban private-sector establishments was approximately:

**RMB 75,838 per person.**

For two ordinary private-sector workers:

**75,838 × 2 = RMB 151,676.**

Using the same method as in Shanghai, we add the employer-side pension, medical, unemployment, work-injury, and related social-insurance costs to labor value.

We keep the same assumptions:

35 years of work;

0% long-run real wage growth;

a 2% real discount rate.

The resulting present value of lifetime labor value for an ordinary Nanjing dual-income household is approximately:

**RMB 4.87 million.**

That RMB 4.87 million is the denominator of the Nanjing model.

Based on Nanjing commodity-housing transaction data around 2021, the central estimate for the average price of a new commodity home is approximately:

**RMB 3.435 million.**

Annual pre-tax household wages are about:

**RMB 151,700.**

Therefore:

**3.435 million ÷ 151,700 ≈ 22.6.**

In other words:

> **An average Nanjing commodity home still cost the equivalent of 22–23 years of the combined pre-tax wages of two ordinary private-sector workers.**

That is much lower than Shanghai's 33 years.

But 22–23 years still means that the couple would have to devote more than two decades of combined pre-tax wages to the home while spending nothing on food, taxes, children, or anything else.

For a strong second-tier city such as Nanjing, the model assumes that:

**land cost + real-estate-related fiscal taxes and fees = 50% of the housing price**

as the central estimate, with a sensitivity range of 45%–55%.

Therefore:

**RMB 3.435 million × 50% ≈ RMB 1.718 million.**

Assume again that the home is purchased in the tenth year of the working life.

Discounted at 2%:

**1.718 million ÷ 1.02¹⁰ ≈ RMB 1.409 million.**

Divide that by lifetime labor value of RMB 4.87 million:

**28.9%.**

So:

> **The land and real-estate fiscal chain embedded in an average Nanjing commodity home is equivalent to about 29% of the lifetime labor value of an ordinary dual-income household.**

Now add the mortgage.

With a 30% down payment, the loan is approximately:

**RMB 2.405 million.**

Around 2021, first-home mortgage rates at some Nanjing banks reached roughly 6% or even higher.

But, as in Shanghai, the model does not assume that this high rate remains in place for 30 years. It incorporates subsequent LPR declines and adjustments to the interest rates on outstanding mortgages.

The resulting total nominal interest over 30 years is approximately:

**RMB 1.50–1.60 million.**

The central estimate is about:

**RMB 1.57 million.**

Discounting the interest by the actual payment schedule and recognizing that the mortgage begins in the tenth year produces a lifecycle present value of approximately:

**RMB 1.07 million.**

Divide that by RMB 4.87 million:

**22.0%.**

The two main housing-related extraction channels in Nanjing are therefore:

Land and real-estate fiscal chain:

**28.9%.**

Mortgage interest:

**22.0%.**

Combined:

**50.9%.**

Shanghai is 79.9%.

Nanjing is almost 30 percentage points lower.

But one important fact remains unchanged:

> **The land-and-housing-finance system alone still consumes the equivalent of roughly half of the lifetime labor value of an ordinary household.**

## 15. Put the other institutional costs back in, and Nanjing still reaches about 71%

Now apply the same remaining components used in the Shanghai model.

Net taxes and social-insurance burden:

**about 14.0%.**

Therefore:

**50.9% + 14.0% = 64.9%.**

Excess education-competition cost:

**the excess of Chinese household education expenditure over the Japanese benchmark, equal to about 1.7% over the full lifecycle.**

That raises the figure to:

**66.6%.**

Central estimate for upstream SOE monopoly and resource misallocation:

**4.0%.**

That raises the figure to:

**70.6%.**

Incremental cost of typical gasoline transportation:

**about 0.8%.**

The final central estimate becomes:

**71.4%.**

The complete Nanjing model is:

| Item | Share of lifetime labor value |
| --- | ---: |
| Net burden of social insurance and taxes | **14.0%** |
| Land and real-estate fiscal chain | **28.9%** |
| Total mortgage interest | **22.0%** |
| Excess education-competition cost | **1.7%** |
| Upstream SOE monopoly/resource-misallocation cost | **4.0%** |
| Incremental cost of typical gasoline transportation | **0.8%** |
| **Total** | **71.4%** |

We now have two cities at very different levels:

> **Shanghai: about 100%**  
> **Nanjing: about 71%**

Shanghai is obviously extreme.

But Nanjing can no longer be dismissed with a single sentence such as:

“That's just what happens in Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and the other first-tier cities.”

Once we leave Shanghai, the number does not collapse back to 20%.

It does not even fall to 40%.

It remains above 70%.

This means we are probably not looking at a simple “Shanghai housing problem.”

The larger question is:

**What happens to the lifetime flow of wealth of ordinary Chinese urban households when land finance, housing finance, compulsory social insurance, education competition, and upstream administrative monopoly are layered on top of one another?**

## 16. And even this 100% is still an extremely conservative figure

This point matters.

When people see 100%, their instinctive reaction is often:

Surely the model has already thrown in every cost it can find.

In fact, the opposite is true.

There are many items that the article knows exist but deliberately does not include.

For example:

**differences in the allocation of medical resources across status groups.**

Special medical treatment for senior officials and other privileged groups has long existed, but because I do not have a sufficiently reliable nationwide total, I do not assign a number to the opportunity cost and resource-allocation loss imposed on ordinary households.

Another example is:

**the pension-treatment gap between public-sector employees and ordinary enterprise workers.**

The pension model uses a 45% retirement-income replacement rate for ordinary formal private-sector workers. It does not separately quantify the extent to which redistribution across status groups may impose an additional burden on ordinary households.

Other excluded items include:

preferential SOE financing and its crowding-out effects on private-sector investment, wages, and employment;

the portion of corporate taxes and fees ultimately shifted onto wages;

profits from the tobacco monopoly;

full-lifecycle expressway tolls;

certain administrative charges;

local-government debt ultimately shifted onto households through future taxation, land prices, or the quality of public services;

and the lifetime income loss caused by mid-career unemployment and prolonged exit from high-quality employment.

These items are not imaginary.

On the contrary, some may be very large.

But if the data are not reliable enough, or if an item cannot be separated cleanly from costs already counted elsewhere, I would rather leave it out than inflate the number through forced inclusion or double counting.

So:

> **100% is not an aggressive upper bound obtained by stuffing every imaginable cost into the model.**

It is a central estimate produced after many known but difficult-to-quantify items have been deliberately excluded.

That is why the most troubling feature of the 100% figure is not that it looks too high.

It is this:

**The model makes numerous conservative choices, and the result still reaches 100%.**

## 17. The question we should be asking is no longer “How high is China's tax burden?”

Return now to the question at the beginning.

Why can conventional statistics create the impression of a burden of only around 20%, while this model reaches 100% in Shanghai and 71% in Nanjing?

Because conventional tax-burden statistics capture only the most visible and easily recorded layer of the broader wealth-transfer structure.

Personal income tax appears on a tax record.

Social-insurance contributions have formal payment records.

Value-added tax appears in fiscal revenue.

Those items therefore enter “tax burden” statistics naturally.

Land finance does not appear on an individual's payslip.

Thirty years of financing cost created by high land and housing prices is not called a tax either.

Education competition does not send parents an invoice labeled “institutional competition tax.”

And welfare losses generated by upstream administrative monopoly do not appear on a supermarket receipt as a separate line reading:

“Upstream monopoly cost: RMB 3.”

But the absence of the word “tax” does not mean that no transfer of wealth has occurred.

That may be the easiest part of the entire problem to overlook.

A large share of the institutional costs borne by ordinary Chinese urban households have never used the word “tax” at all.

So a person can spend an entire working life thinking:

“My personal income tax is not very high.”

At the same time, that person can discover that:

a home costs decades of combined household wages;

parents must hand over a lifetime of savings for the down payment;

the younger generation then assumes another 30 years of mortgage debt;

once children arrive, the family continues paying into education competition;

and even as nominal wages rise, the household never feels able to expand consumption with confidence.

There is no contradiction.

The largest channels of wealth extraction may never have been located mainly in the personal-income-tax line.

## 18. What does 100% actually mean?

The most important meaning of the Shanghai model's 100.2% result is not:

“Shanghai households are left with nothing.”

Nor is it:

“The Shanghai government directly takes 100% of household wages.”

Both interpretations are wrong.

What the result means is:

> **For the specific Shanghai household defined in this article—an ordinary dual-income private-sector household that buys an average commodity home with typical leverage—the identifiable direct levies and institutionally generated indirect extraction, when converted into the same lifecycle labor-value denominator, reach a central estimate roughly equal to the entire labor value created by that generation.**

And once a one-generation model can reach 100%, a larger question appears immediately:

**How does that generation's household balance sheet close at all?**

The answer is becoming increasingly obvious.

It is not a one-generation household model.

It is a:

**three-generation household model.**

Parents provide the down payment.

Grandparents leave savings.

Housing appreciation from an earlier property cycle becomes stock wealth.

The young couple then uses the next 30 years of labor income to service the mortgage.

That is why the once-popular Chinese expression—

**“six wallets”**

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/littlefamily-01.jpg" alt="littlefamily-supplied-by-the-family" /></figure>

—is more accurate than it may first appear.

It may not merely describe a family culture.

It may be the balance-sheet solution that naturally emerges when one generation's labor income can no longer independently finance the purchase of housing.

When the current generation's labor income is not enough, the household draws on the previous generation.

If that is still not enough, it draws on housing assets and family wealth accumulated even earlier.

So the next question worth studying is no longer simply:

“How much tax does one person pay?”

It is not even merely:

“How much is extracted from one household over a lifetime?”

The more important question becomes:

> **For an urban Chinese family across three generations, out of every 100 yuan of wealth created, how much is ultimately transferred out of the household sector through these institutional channels?**

The next model should therefore be built around:

**grandparents → parents → children**

and place the down payment, intergenerational transfers, land finance, mortgage interest, pensions, healthcare, education, residual housing value, asset-price changes, and final inheritance on the same balance sheet.

Because if the Shanghai model is even approximately right, then the true story of urban household wealth in China over the past several decades may never have been simply:

“How much did this generation earn?”

The real question is:

> **Through what mechanisms has the wealth created jointly by several generations been redistributed among government, the financial system, the state-owned sector, and ordinary households?**

Only after that balance sheet is reconstructed can we answer the most basic question:

**Of all the wealth created during China's decades of rapid economic growth, how much has actually accumulated in ordinary household balance sheets?**
