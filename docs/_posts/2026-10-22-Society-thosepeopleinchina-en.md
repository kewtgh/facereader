I can tell whether someone lives in mainland China just by looking at one of their online comments.

I suspect many people on the internet have had the same impression.

Among the many mainland Chinese netizens I have encountered, one common trait is the anger in their words, as though the whole world bears them ill will.

Faced with a different opinion, they often question the other person's motives before even understanding what has been said. An ordinary discussion can quickly turn into a battle between friends and enemies.

Others behave in quite the opposite way. They seem to have seen through everything, suppressing their concern for the people around them and refusing to engage in arguments. Yet this detachment sometimes feels less than genuine. There is an affected air of withdrawal in the way they speak, as though not caring about reality could somehow make its problems irrelevant to them.

I have encountered similar people abroad, but in my experience, these two patterns are more pronounced in some mainland Chinese online communities.

Recently, a friend living in mainland China told me about his distress. It was not simply dissatisfaction with everyday life, but a persistent sense of despondency brought on by political reality. He used an English word to describe his state: Depression.

Some of my other friends feel much the same way. They are not necessarily interested in politics, nor do they regularly discuss public affairs. But whenever certain aspects of Chinese society come up, they show a deep sense of helplessness. They can see the problems and form their own judgments, yet find few ways to change the reality around them.

I think people who are more prone to political depression are often more willing to think for themselves and more concerned about injustice. They have their own judgments about reality, and they find it difficult to turn a blind eye to things that violate their values.

This made me wonder whether the anger and detachment I described earlier might be two ways some people cope with political depression.

Some vent their frustration by attacking others. Others try to convince themselves to stop caring about things they cannot change.

Their behavior looks nothing like that of someone suffering from political depression, but that does not mean reality has left them unaffected.

## 1. Why Do Some People See Hostility in Everyone Else?

Why, then, are some Chinese netizens so quick to interpret other people's behavior as a personal attack?

For example, criticizing a policy may be taken as an attack on China. Questioning a social phenomenon may lead to accusations of ulterior motives. Even expressing a different opinion can invite immediate insults.

**These people seem far more concerned with which side someone is on than with what that person actually says.**

**Psychology has a term for this: *Hostile Attribution Bias*.**

> It refers to the tendency to interpret another person's ambiguous behavior as hostile. A systematic review and meta-analysis published in 2026 also found a significant association between trait anger and hostile attribution bias.

I think this concept helps explain some of the arguments we see online.

**If someone spends a long time in an information environment filled with distrust, divisions between friends and enemies, and mutual attacks, they may gradually learn to interpret other people in the same way.**

A different opinion is no longer just a different opinion. It may be taken as proof that the other person belongs to a hostile group.

Someone who tries harder to explain their position may even be suspected of trying to conceal their true intentions.

But where does this state of mind come from?

I tend to think that some of it may be connected to frustration and helplessness in everyday life.

A person may be dissatisfied with their income, job, housing, or other social conditions, yet lack the ability to change them. If they also have no effective way to clarify who is responsible through public discussion, that frustration may gradually build up.

**But anger needs a target.**

**Psychology describes a phenomenon known as *Displaced Aggression*:**

> **When someone cannot express anger toward the source of their frustration, they may redirect it toward another target who is easier to attack.**

For example, someone may be treated unfairly by a supervisor but remain silent for fear of losing their job. After coming home, they may lash out at family members over something trivial.

The supervisor caused the distress, but the family ends up bearing the anger.

A similar psychological mechanism may also appear in social life.

Someone who cannot influence the real problems they face may nevertheless spend their time attacking Americans, Japanese people, Taiwanese people, or strangers who disagree with them online.

They do not have to bear the risks of changing reality, yet condemning others can give them a certain psychological satisfaction.

**More troublingly, repeated attacks can reinforce their hostility.**

**They attack others, and those people may naturally fight back. This makes them even more convinced that everyone around them is malicious. The next time they encounter a different opinion, they become angry all the more easily.**

Eventually, even when the other person has no intention of attacking them, their first instinct is to be suspicious.

**Someone who was originally dissatisfied with reality may gradually become a person who sees hostility in everyone around them.**

## 2. Another Response: Trying to Become Detached

Compared with netizens who always seem ready to attack others, I find another group even more worth examining.

They often appear remarkably calm, as though they have already seen through everything.

**They do not want to discuss public affairs and show little concern for what happens to others. When someone expresses dissatisfaction with society, they may tell that person not to overthink things, or mock those who still care about politics.**

**But I do not think this air of detachment necessarily comes from genuine peace of mind.**

When someone first encounters injustice, they may feel angry or saddened by it.

**But when similar things keep happening and they discover they can do nothing to change them, paying continued attention becomes a psychological burden.**

**So they begin to pay less attention, stop discussing these matters, and even deliberately avoid news that makes them uncomfortable.**

This is not difficult to understand.

People have limited energy. There is no need to exhaust oneself over every problem one cannot solve.

But for some, the change does not stop there.

They not only stop caring; they also begin looking for reasons to justify their indifference.

They may tell themselves that this is simply how society works, that ordinary people have no reason to get involved in politics, or that those who suffer injustice just were not smart enough.

**Eventually, they may even begin to resent people who still care about social problems.**

**Those people remind them of judgments they once held but have worked hard to suppress.**

**The psychological concept of *Cognitive Dissonance* helps explain this change.**

> When a person's behavior conflicts with their original values, they may ease their discomfort by reinterpreting their behavior or even changing their original judgments.

At first, someone may remain silent simply out of fear.

Later, they begin telling themselves that silence is the rational choice.

Eventually, they may genuinely believe that people who still dare to express different opinions simply have not understood reality.

That way, they no longer have to feel uneasy about their own silence.

So when I speak of false detachment, I do not mean everyone who is uninterested in politics. I mean those who, in an effort to escape their inner conflict, gradually convince themselves not to care, sometimes even to the point of denying the suffering of others.

I find this psychological change deeply troubling.

Remaining silent to protect oneself and gradually coming to believe that other people's suffering does not matter are two very different things.

## 3. Why Are Some People Perfectly at Ease?

There is another kind of person we need to consider.

They seem to experience neither political depression nor any need to manage their emotions through anger or deliberate detachment.

They are quite satisfied with reality and may even believe that many of society's injustices are justified.

I used to think that when someone knows about the suffering around them yet feels no unease, and even willingly defends things that are plainly unreasonable, there are roughly two explanations:

- One is an unwillingness to think. They make no serious effort to understand how things happened and have no desire to question the explanations they have accepted.

- The other is a lack of compassion for others' suffering. They understand what has happened but see no reason to care as long as their own interests are unharmed.

**To put it harshly, one is foolish, and the other is bad.**

I know this is a sharp judgment. But I am not judging someone's conscience by whether they are happy. A person can understand the injustices around them and still lead a good life.

**What I am criticizing is a different attitude: when faced with real harm, a person is unwilling either to understand the facts or to care about those who have been hurt.**

If someone lacks even basic compassion, we can hardly expect them to suffer over another person's misfortune.

Conversely, someone who is willing to think and who retains compassion for others may experience greater psychological conflict when faced with the same reality.

They know certain things are wrong, yet cannot dismiss them as irrelevant simply because they themselves have not been harmed.

This may be an important starting point for understanding political depression.

## 4. Political Depression: When Someone Cannot Convince Themselves

**Psychology and ethics have a concept known as *Moral Distress*.**

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/Melancholy Apartment Under the Watchful Skyline.jpg" alt="Melancholy Apartment Under the Watchful Skyline" /></figure>

> Originally used mainly in medical ethics, the term describes the distress someone experiences when they believe they know what the right thing to do is but are prevented by institutional or practical constraints from acting according to their moral judgment.

For example, a doctor may know that a particular decision could harm a patient but be unable to change the outcome because of hospital rules or power relationships.

**The doctor's distress comes not only from the harm done to the patient, but also from being unable to act according to their conscience.**

**Political life can produce a similar psychological conflict.**

Someone may recognize problems with the exercise of public power, see people treated unfairly, or discover that certain public narratives plainly contradict the facts.

They may have their own judgments, yet lack the space to express them or act upon them.

**When this continues for a long time, the pain may no longer come from any single event. It may gradually become part of how they experience the entire social environment.**

I think this is one important difference between people suffering from political depression and the two groups described earlier.

- Those filled with hostility may gain temporary psychological satisfaction by attacking others.

- Those who try to appear detached may ease their distress by paying less attention and reinterpreting reality.

- Those experiencing political depression may be unable to do either.

  They cannot simply redirect their dissatisfaction with reality toward other people, nor can they convince themselves that the things they consider wrong do not really matter.

The result is that they retain their original values while having to live with a persistent conflict between those values and reality.

This suffering does not prove that someone is morally superior, nor should it be treated as a badge of conscience.

But it does remind us that concern for society can become a heavy psychological burden when it cannot be translated into any effective action.

## 5. Why Do These States of Mind Appear on Mainland China's Internet?

If we look only at individual psychology, we still cannot explain why certain emotions appear so repeatedly in a particular online environment.

We also need to consider the political and information environment in mainland China.

In 2013, Gary King, Jennifer Pan, and Margaret Roberts published a study of internet censorship in China.

> They found that, in the online environment they studied, criticism of the government did not necessarily make a post more likely to be censored. Content that could encourage people to connect with one another and engage in collective action was more likely to be targeted.

This means that expressing dissatisfaction and building connections around shared grievances in order to bring about change are two different things.

> In 2017, the same research team further found that the government-organized online comments they studied mainly sought to divert public attention through positive messaging and changes of subject, rather than directly arguing with critics.

These studies cannot directly explain the origins of every online emotion today, but they show how an information environment can influence the direction of public discussion.

**If someone finds it difficult to have an effective discussion about problems in their own life, but is free to express anger on other issues, they may gradually become accustomed to directing their frustration toward the latter.**

**And platforms may reinforce that habit.**

> A study published in 2024 examined more than 500,000 comments on Tencent News during the 2012 Diaoyu Islands dispute. It found significant associations between news framing, user participation, emotion, and expressions of online nationalism. Platforms could amplify nationalist sentiment, but they could also restrict it when they feared the emotions might get out of control.

So we cannot simply assume that the anger of mainland Chinese netizens is entirely manufactured by political propaganda. Much of their frustration comes from real life, and platforms cannot completely determine how people feel.

But what someone can see over a long period, which emotions receive approval, and which questions are difficult to discuss openly can all influence how that person expresses themselves.

Someone may have no opportunity to influence a public decision that directly affects their life, yet can spend hours online condemning a distant country.

They can express intense anger, but may have little opportunity to discuss why they are dissatisfied in the first place.

Others may gradually grow tired of living this way.

They no longer want to join arguments, and they no longer expect discussion to change anything. To avoid disappointment, they begin deliberately paying less attention to social problems.

The result is two seemingly opposite reactions: some people become increasingly quick to anger, while others work harder and harder to appear detached.

Neither response necessarily comes from political depression. But for people who once cared about reality and later came to feel powerless, they may indeed offer two different kinds of psychological escape.

## 6. An Uncomfortable Judgment

As I write this, I think again of the friend I mentioned at the beginning.

He suffers because of a reality he cannot change. Meanwhile, some people living in the same environment are angrily attacking others online, while others are trying hard to appear as though they care about nothing at all.

I think these phenomena may be more closely connected than they first appear.

When someone has their own judgment about injustice and retains compassion for the suffering of others, dissatisfaction is a natural response.

If that dissatisfaction cannot be addressed through effective public discussion and action, the person has to find some other way to deal with it.

- Some direct their dissatisfaction outward, attacking others to release their anger.

- Some suppress their concern and gradually convince themselves to stop paying attention to reality.

- Others can do neither and must continue living with their inner pain.

- And some, of course, never gave these problems serious thought in the first place, or simply do not care about what happens to other people.

These people may appear similar, but the psychological reasons behind their behavior are not necessarily the same.

**What truly worries me is whether someone who once thought independently and cared about others might, after years of helplessness and frustration, gradually become hostile to everyone around them—or indifferent to the suffering of others.**

During that process, the reality that caused their dissatisfaction may have changed very little.

What has changed is the person.

**If a society makes it increasingly difficult for people to change reality through normal public discussion and action, the cost may be borne not only by those who fall into political depression, but also by those who have learned to stop caring about others—or even to hate them.**

And that may deserve more attention than the number of angry people we see online.

## 7. A Man's Compromise in Grossman's Fiction

Finally, I am reminded of *Life and Fate*, by the Soviet writer Vasily Grossman.

One of the characters is a physicist named Viktor Shtrum.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/Wartime Scholar Beneath Searchlights.jpg" alt="Wartime Scholar Beneath Searchlights" /></figure>

Shtrum is a gifted scientist with his own ideas and judgments. He is also reluctant to change his academic position merely to satisfy political demands.

But in Stalin's Soviet Union, such a temperament could easily get him into trouble.

As political pressure mounts, Shtrum is pushed aside, his research is obstructed, and even people who were once close to him begin to distance themselves. He starts to fear that he may be arrested.

Then something almost unbelievable happens.

Stalin himself calls him on the telephone.

That phone call completely changes Shtrum's situation.

People who had shunned him begin approaching him again. His research receives support, his standing is restored, and his life becomes safe once more.

In that era, a single gesture of favor from the supreme leader could change a person's fate.

But Grossman's most profound observation comes afterward.

Shtrum is asked to sign a public letter condemning others.

He knows the accusations are flawed and understands what his signature would mean. He does not agree with the contents of the letter.

Yet faced with the career, status, and sense of security he has just regained, along with the renewed friendliness of those around him, he signs it.

I find this episode even more revealing than the political ostracism he suffered earlier.

Shtrum is not a man incapable of judgment.

When he was isolated and facing political danger, he had tried to stand by his principles. But once his life returned to normal, and he again had a career and security worth protecting, he compromised.

Why?

Because now he had more to lose.

When someone has already lost almost everything, it may actually be easier to stand by their principles. Once they have regained their career, status, and ordinary life, however, refusing to cooperate means risking the loss of those things all over again.

More troublingly, this time no one even has to threaten him directly.

He can imagine the consequences of refusing for himself.

So he signs.

But that is not the end of it.

Shtrum regains his safety, but not his peace of mind.

Because he knows what he has done.

He did not sign because he believed the accusations, or because he did not know the truth. He knew perfectly well that he had betrayed the moral judgment he once upheld.

It is precisely this awareness that fills him with shame.

Grossman does not portray Shtrum as simply a coward or a bad man.

He remains thoughtful, conscientious, and capable of distinguishing right from wrong.

But when his livelihood, safety, and career came under threat, he ultimately failed to stand by his principles.

That is what makes this character so painful to read about.

We can readily understand why someone suffers when they are persecuted.

But Grossman describes another kind of suffering: **a man adapts to power and gains security in the outside world, yet loses his peace of mind before his own conscience.**

He can explain to others that he did it to survive, to protect his family, and to continue his work.

These reasons are not entirely without merit.

But being able to explain one's behavior does not mean being able to accept it.

Other people may have stopped asking questions, or may never have known what he went through internally. But he knows.

**Sometimes, what a person finds hardest to bear is not that others will not let him tell the truth, but that he has begun to tell lies of his own accord.**

Today's China and the Soviet Union under Stalin are, of course, separated by enormous historical differences. The two cannot simply be equated.

I am not citing Grossman to apply the political persecution of one era directly to the present.

What interests me is the aspect of human nature he depicts.

**When power can determine a person's safety, career, and social standing, its influence goes beyond prohibition and coercion.**

**It can also change the price a person must pay for making a moral choice.**

**A person may retain their original conscience yet find it increasingly difficult to act according to it.**

**And the compromises they make to adapt to reality may themselves become a new source of suffering.**
