At the end of the [Finland article](https://facereader.witbacon.com/社会杂论/Society-lcer-finland/), I said that we would turn our attention to Asia next. We will begin with Japan.

Using the same household model as the rest of this series, with 2021 as the base year, the estimated Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate (LCER) is **approximately 23.8% for Tokyo and 21.2% for Nagoya.**[^method]

Pension and health insurance contributions in Japan are substantial, and housing prices in Tokyo are considerably higher than in Nagoya. But once we account for pensions received after retirement, healthcare benefits, and the cost of financing a home over the years, taxes and social insurance still account for most of the result. Housing does widen the gap between the two cities. Its share of this lifetime bill, however, is smaller than the full price of a home might suggest.

We can start with the family's income and work through how these figures emerge.

## 1. The Same Family, Living in Tokyo or Nagoya

As before, we assume a couple who both work full time in the private sector and raise one child. They begin working at 25 and retire at 65. Long-term real wage growth is set at 0%, and the real discount rate at 2%, consistent with the common assumptions used in the preceding articles. The 0% assumption holds the conditions of comparison constant; it is not a forecast of future wage growth in Japan.

Tokyo is the leading city, while Nagoya is the representative city. Wages for both are drawn from the regional data in the same official wage survey: Tokyo uses the Tokyo Metropolis figures, while Nagoya uses Aichi Prefecture as a proxy.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-lcer-Tokyo Family Life.jpg" alt="ai-lcer-Tokyo Family Life" /></figure>

Multiplying regular monthly cash earnings by 12 and adding annual bonuses and other special payments gives estimated annual earnings of about ¥5.849 million per worker in Tokyo and ¥5.240 million in Nagoya.[^wages]

For the two spouses combined, annual household cash earnings are approximately ¥11.70 million in Tokyo and ¥10.48 million in Nagoya. An “ordinary family” here means a standardized household with two full-time earners, each earning the regional average wage. These figures are not the median income of all local households, nor should they be treated as the actual starting salary of a 25-year-old.

Following the approach used throughout this series, labor value also includes the social insurance and other contributions that employers must pay for these two workers. In this model, mandatory employer contributions amount to approximately 15.8% of cash earnings. Annual household labor value is therefore about ¥13.55 million in Tokyo and ¥12.14 million in Nagoya.

Discounting 40 years of labor value back to the common starting point of age 25 gives the following results:[^present-value]

| Household income and labor value | Tokyo | Nagoya |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Annual cash earnings per worker | Approx. ¥5.849 million | Approx. ¥5.240 million |
| Annual household cash earnings | Approx. ¥11.70 million | Approx. ¥10.48 million |
| Annual labor value, including mandatory employer contributions | Approx. ¥13.55 million | Approx. ¥12.14 million |
| Present value of lifetime labor value | Approx. ¥370.61 million | Approx. ¥332.13 million |

The pension, healthcare, tax, and housing burdens calculated below all use these two lifetime labor values as their denominators. A contribution rate expressed as a percentage of wages is therefore not directly equivalent to the number of percentage points it contributes to LCER.

## 2. Pensions: Contributing from 25 to 65, Then Counting the Years of Retirement

Japan's Employees' Pension Insurance has a combined employer and employee contribution rate of 18.3%, with each side paying half. The employee's share is what appears directly on the payslip, but this series includes both sides of the contribution.[^employees-pension]

At the wages and working lives assumed here, the present value of lifetime pension contributions is approximately ¥58.56 million for the Tokyo family and ¥52.47 million for the Nagoya family. That is a substantial outlay. But it is only half of the calculation: the pensions received after retirement must be deducted.

Drawing on the OECD's pension model for Japan and adjusting for the wage levels in the two regions, this estimate uses a gross pension replacement rate of approximately 31%–32%. This is the ratio of the pension before tax to earnings before tax prior to retirement, and it is an estimated parameter used in this calculation.[^replacement-rate]

At that level, each person's annual pension before tax would be a little over ¥1.8 million in Tokyo and a little over ¥1.6 million in Nagoya.

We also need to consider how long those payments will continue.

Japan's 2021 life tables, published by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, show that a man who has already reached 65 can expect to live another 19.85 years, and a woman another 24.73 years. These correspond to ages of approximately 84.9 and 89.7, respectively.[^remaining-life] For this family retiring at 65, pension payments can therefore be expected to continue for around two decades, and longer for the wife.

Discounting the estimated annual pensions over the two spouses' expected retirement periods back to age 25 gives pension benefits with a present value of approximately ¥29.55 million in Tokyo and ¥27.05 million in Nagoya. After deducting these benefits, the results are:

| Pension component, all in lifetime present values | Tokyo | Nagoya |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Combined employer and employee contributions | Approx. ¥58.56 million | Approx. ¥52.47 million |
| Pension benefits received after retirement | Approx. ¥29.55 million | Approx. ¥27.05 million |
| Net extraction after deducting benefits | Approx. ¥29.01 million | Approx. ¥25.42 million |
| Share of lifetime labor value | Approx. 7.8% | Approx. 7.7% |

A contribution rate of 18.3% thus corresponds to lifetime net extraction of approximately 7.7%–7.8%. The difference reflects the benefits received, as well as the different denominator and the timing of payments. The 18.3% rate applies to earnings subject to contributions, whereas LCER's denominator also includes other mandatory labor costs borne by employers. Pensions received decades later must also be discounted using the same rules.

This difference measures the gap between the standard family's contributions and the benefits attributable to it under the stated assumptions. Pension insurance also protects against longevity risk and redistributes resources. The gap cannot simply be interpreted as an equivalent amount of institutional waste.

## 3. Healthcare: Paying During Working Life, Using More in Old Age

Health insurance has a similar pattern over time. A household pays substantial premiums during its working years, while its use of healthcare changes with age. Looking only at how much people pay and how much is reimbursed in their thirties or forties can easily leave out the protection they receive later in life.

In 2021, the Japan Health Insurance Association's health insurance contribution rate was 9.84% in Tokyo and 9.91% in Aichi. These are also combined employer and employee rates, not percentages deducted entirely from the employee's wages. Other contribution arrangements apply at different ages and under different insurance schemes.[^health-insurance]

On the benefits side, Japan provides statistics broken down by age. Using 2021 data, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare estimated lifetime medical expenditure from birth at approximately ¥26.98 million per person, of which about ¥23 million would be covered by health insurance benefits.[^medical-benefits] These figures have not been discounted, and they are not amounts that a 25-year-old adult is certain to receive in the future. They do, however, provide a basis for estimating healthcare benefits by age.

Using the age profile, this model discounts the health insurance benefits attributable to the household back to age 25, producing a present value of approximately ¥19.24 million. Both cities use the same household structure and healthcare benefit estimate, while contributions vary with wages and other conditions:

| Healthcare component, all in lifetime present values | Tokyo | Nagoya |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Health insurance contributions and premiums | Approx. ¥32.68 million | Approx. ¥29.45 million |
| Health insurance benefits attributable to the household | Approx. ¥19.24 million | Approx. ¥19.24 million |
| Net extraction after deducting benefits | Approx. ¥13.44 million | Approx. ¥10.22 million |
| Share of lifetime labor value | Approx. 3.6% | Approx. 3.1% |

Tokyo's health insurance contribution rate is slightly lower than Aichi's, so why does its net extraction end up higher? Because the Tokyo household in the model earns more and therefore pays more in wage-based contributions, while the model assigns the same amount of healthcare benefits to both households. Higher premiums do not translate into proportionately more healthcare services.

This shows why comparing healthcare burdens requires us to examine both how contributions are calculated and how benefits are allocated. Two households facing similar contribution rates can end up with different gaps between contributions and benefits because their incomes differ.

## 4. How Income Tax and Consumption Tax Enter the Bill

For personal income tax, taxable income is first calculated after applying the employment income deduction, social insurance deductions, the basic deduction, and other applicable rules. National income tax and local resident taxes are then calculated, including the mortgage tax credit available under the home-purchase scenario used in this model.[^income-tax]

Using the 2021 parameters, the estimated present value of lifetime income tax and resident taxes is approximately ¥25.05 million for the Tokyo household, or 6.8% of lifetime labor value. For Nagoya, it is approximately ¥20.57 million, or 6.2%. Higher wages in Tokyo bring a higher burden under the progressive income tax system.

Consumption tax requires a different calculation. In 2021, Japan's standard consumption tax rate was 10%, with a reduced rate of 8% applying to eligible food and certain other purchases.[^consumption-tax] But household earnings are not spent entirely on one kind of taxable consumption, and income set aside as savings does not automatically incur consumption tax. We therefore cannot multiply all earnings by 10%, or simply add another 10 percentage points to LCER.

Starting with the household consumption budget and distinguishing between spending subject to different rates and exempt spending, this estimate puts lifetime consumption tax at a present value of approximately ¥10.15 million in Tokyo and ¥10.08 million in Nagoya. These represent 2.7% and 3.0% of lifetime labor value, respectively.

The consumption tax amounts are close, yet Nagoya's percentage is higher. The main reason is the denominator: the Nagoya household has a lower lifetime labor value, so a similar expense takes a larger share of its income. This works in the opposite direction to income tax, and the distinction needs to be preserved when the figures are combined.

Adding net pension and healthcare extraction brings the fiscal and social insurance total to approximately ¥77.65 million in Tokyo, or 21.0%, and ¥66.27 million in Nagoya, or 20.0%. This already accounts for most of Japan's LCER.

Other social insurance, including unemployment, workers' compensation, and long-term care insurance, is provisionally assigned a net amount of zero in this central estimate. This does not mean that the contributions themselves do not exist.[^other-insurance]

## 5. How Much Do Tokyo's Housing Prices Add to the Institutional Burden?

We now turn to housing, where the difference between Tokyo and Nagoya is most pronounced.

According to Tokyo Kantei, the 2021 annual average resale apartment price, standardized to 70 square meters, was approximately ¥63.33 million in Tokyo's 23 wards and ¥24.96 million in Nagoya City.[^home-prices] Tokyo's modeled wage is about 12% higher than Nagoya's, but an apartment of the same size costs approximately 2.54 times as much.

| Housing and household income | Tokyo | Nagoya |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Resale apartment price, standardized to 70 square meters | ¥63.33 million | ¥24.96 million |
| Home price / annual household cash earnings | Approx. 5.4 times | Approx. 2.4 times |
| Down payment at 30% | Approx. ¥19.00 million | Approx. ¥7.49 million |
| Mortgage at 70% | Approx. ¥44.33 million | Approx. ¥17.47 million |

Even without considering LCER, the down payment alone shows that homebuyers in the two cities face different financial pressures. But in this calculation, part of the money paid for a home becomes an asset held by the family. The full purchase price cannot simply be counted as institutional extraction. We have already discussed this distinction in the articles on China, the United States, and Europe.

We therefore need to identify which parts of the price can be attributed to institutional arrangements, along with the specific taxes and charges payable after the purchase.

This estimate does not add a housing regulatory premium for Tokyo or Nagoya where reliable quantification is still lacking. A high price by itself does not tell us how much is due to planning restrictions and how much to location or other market factors.

What can be clearly identified are the fixed asset tax and city planning tax paid over the years of homeownership. Japan's standard fixed asset tax rate is 1.4%, and the maximum city planning tax rate is 0.3%. These taxes are based on assessed values, however, and residential land is eligible for relief. For qualifying small residential plots, for example, the taxable bases for fixed asset tax and city planning tax can be reduced to one-sixth and one-third of the assessed value, respectively.[^property-tax]

Adding 1.4% and 0.3% and then multiplying the result by the home's full market price would therefore overstate this burden. Conversely, relief for residential land cannot simply be applied to the entire value of the building.

The model uses an estimated long-term effective property holding tax equal to 0.30% of the home's market value per year. This is a proxy assumption allowing for valuation and relief, not a uniform effective rate published by the Japanese authorities. Assuming a purchase in the tenth year of working life, with holding taxes paid from the following year until the surviving spouse reaches approximately 90, their present value is about ¥5.17 million in Tokyo and ¥2.04 million in Nagoya. These amount to 1.4% and 0.6% of lifetime labor value, respectively.

Taxes associated with buying a home, including real estate acquisition tax, registration and license tax, and stamp duty, have not yet been incorporated individually according to the property's assessed value and applicable relief. They are not included in the ¥5.17 million and ¥2.04 million figures above.

## 6. How a 1.1% Mortgage Rate Affects the Long-Term Cost of Borrowing

Once the home price is established, mortgage interest still needs to be calculated.

Historical survey results published by the Japan Housing Finance Agency show reference average borrowing rates of 1.0% and 1.2% in its April and October 2021 surveys, respectively. This model takes the midpoint of 1.1%, with a mortgage covering 70% of the purchase price and repaid over 30 years in equal installments of principal and interest.[^mortgage]

At this rate, the interest generated by a mortgage of approximately ¥44.33 million in Tokyo has a present value, discounted back to age 25, of about ¥5.22 million. Nagoya's mortgage of approximately ¥17.47 million produces interest with a present value of about ¥2.06 million. These represent 1.4% and 0.6% of household lifetime labor value, respectively. Only interest is included here; repayment of the mortgage principal is not counted again as extraction.

Combining property holding taxes and mortgage interest, housing-related components add approximately 2.8 percentage points in Tokyo and 1.2 percentage points in Nagoya. The Tokyo family needs a larger down payment and takes on more debt, but under the interest rate assumed here, the additional cost of borrowing remains limited.

This also explains why a high home price does not always mean that housing accounts for a large share of LCER. The former includes the value of the asset acquired by the family; the latter depends on identifiable institutional costs, the amount borrowed, the interest rate, and the repayment schedule. Even at the same home price, different financing terms can change the amount a household pays for borrowing over its lifetime.

The 1.1% rate is a parameter in the 2021 baseline scenario, applied throughout the repayment period. It is not a forecast for the next 30 years, nor does it mean that every household at the time obtained the same fixed-rate mortgage. The mortgage results in this article should be read with that assumption in mind.

## 7. What Does a Zero Education Component Mean?

Japan's excess education competition spending is zero in this estimate, and that requires a separate explanation. Otherwise, a modeling assumption can easily be mistaken for a claim about education spending and competition among Japanese families.

This series uses 1.5% of household consumption as the baseline for normal private education spending. Japan serves as the calibration reference for that baseline, and the Japanese model here also uses 1.5%. The difference is therefore zero.

<figure class="fr-figure"><img src="https://kewtgh.github.io/PicSunflowers/img/2026/ai-lcer-Tokyo Family Moments in Spring.jpg" alt="ai-lcer-Tokyo Family Moments in Spring" /></figure>

As discussed in the Finland article, when education spending falls below the baseline, the negative difference should be retained. The common calculation subtracts 1.5% from the local share of household consumption spent on education, multiplies the difference by the corresponding household consumption budget, discounts the expenditure over the years in which it actually occurs for one child, and finally divides by lifetime labor value.[^education]

Japan's zero therefore means only that the education spending assumed here matches the comparison baseline. It does not establish that every family raising a child in Tokyo or Nagoya spends exactly 1.5% of its consumption budget on education, or that educational competition does not exist.

## 8. What Do 23.8% and 21.2% Ultimately Tell Us?

Putting the components together gives the following results. All percentages use household lifetime labor value as the denominator, and pension and health insurance benefits attributable to the household have already been deducted.

| Component | Tokyo | Nagoya |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Net pension extraction | 7.8% | 7.7% |
| Net health insurance extraction | 3.6% | 3.1% |
| Personal income tax and resident taxes | 6.8% | 6.2% |
| Consumption tax | 2.7% | 3.0% |
| Fiscal and social insurance subtotal | 21.0% | 20.0% |
| Property holding taxes | 1.4% | 0.6% |
| Mortgage interest | 1.4% | 0.6% |
| Excess education competition spending | 0.0% | 0.0% |
| **Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate** | **23.8%** | **21.2%** |

Each figure is rounded separately, so the displayed components may not sum exactly to the subtotals or totals.

In this estimate, approximately ¥88.04 million of the Tokyo household's ¥370.61 million in lifetime labor value is counted as net extraction. For Nagoya, the corresponding amount is approximately ¥70.37 million out of ¥332.13 million.

Of the approximately 2.6-percentage-point gap between Tokyo and Nagoya, about 1.6 percentage points come from property holding taxes and mortgage interest. Most of the remainder comes from fiscal and social insurance components. Higher wages in Tokyo partly offset the effect of higher expenses, but the housing price gap is much greater than the wage gap, so housing still pushes up the Tokyo family's LCER.

Excise taxes on fuel, alcohol, and tobacco, along with some of the housing transaction taxes discussed earlier, have not been fully included. Nor are there sufficiently reliable household-level amounts for costs arising from housing regulation, public monopolies, or resource misallocation. The figures of 23.8% and 21.2% are therefore estimates for the components identified by this model, not upper limits on all potential institutional costs.[^coverage]

LCER also cannot, by itself, tell us whether a family's life is financially comfortable. The remainder still has to cover everyday consumption, mortgage principal, and other household expenses. Working hours, income stability, and the prospect of a better life in the future require separate consideration.

What this calculation shows for Japan is that taxes and social insurance remain the main burden after pension and healthcare benefits are deducted. Tokyo's higher housing prices add pressure, but under the low interest rate and property holding tax assumptions used here, housing does not become the dominant part of the bill. Examining these specific payments and the benefits received in return allows us to judge a family's institutional burden more accurately than looking only at a payslip or a home price.

## Notes

[^method]: LCER stands for the Lifecycle Comprehensive Extraction Rate used in this series. The denominator is the present value of lifetime household cash earnings plus mandatory employer labor costs. The numerator includes the taxes and charges identified by the model; mandatory social insurance contributions less benefits attributable to the household; institutional housing costs; mortgage interest; and the difference between education competition spending and the baseline. The present values and the results of 23.8% and 21.2% come from this series' Japanese calculation. They are estimates for a standardized household, not official Japanese statistics on the overall household burden.

[^wages]: See the Miyagi Labour Bureau's [Wage Conditions in Miyagi, May 2022](https://jsite.mhlw.go.jp/miyagi-roudoukyoku/content/contents/002059239.pdf), pages 25–27, which reproduce results from the 2021 Basic Survey on Wage Structure. Tokyo Metropolis records regular monthly cash earnings of ¥391,800 and annual bonuses and other special payments of ¥1,147,700; the corresponding Aichi Prefecture figures are ¥354,600 and ¥985,200. Monthly earnings refer to June 2021, while bonuses refer to the full calendar year 2020. This article annualizes those figures; they should not be interpreted as the actual total income earned by the same workers during calendar year 2021. Regional average earnings for general workers are used as proxies for the standard household; Nagoya does not have a separate city-level wage sample here.

[^present-value]: Present value (PV) converts amounts occurring at different times to a common starting point using a consistent discount rate. This article uses age 25 as that starting point and a real discount rate of 2%. “Real” means that amounts are treated in terms of constant purchasing power. Lifetime labor value is neither the simple sum of 40 years of wages nor the balance in a bank account at retirement. The 0% real wage growth assumption is a common condition used for comparison.

[^employees-pension]: Employees' Pension Insurance is Japan's public pension scheme for employees. Contributions are calculated from standard monthly remuneration and standard bonuses, with employers and employees each paying half. The combined rate has been fixed at 18.3% since September 2017. See the [Japan Pension Service's explanation of Employees' Pension Insurance contributions](https://www.nenkin.go.jp/service/kounen/hokenryo/hoshu/20150515-01.html). This model simplifies contributions to a percentage of wages rather than reproducing every remuneration band individually.

[^replacement-rate]: The gross pension replacement rate is the ratio of the pension before tax to earnings before tax prior to retirement. The OECD's Pensions at a Glance 2021 reports a rate of 32.4% for an average earner in Japan. This model uses proxy parameters of approximately 31%–32%, allowing for wages in the two regions. The original OECD model assumes entry into work at age 22 in 2020 and annual real wage growth of 1.25%, unlike this article's assumptions of entry at 25 and 0% real wage growth. This is therefore not a year-by-year actuarial calculation of statutory Japanese pension entitlements. See [OECD Table 4.1 on replacement rates](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2021_ca401ebd-en/full-report/component-16.html) and the [methodology and assumptions](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2021_ca401ebd-en/full-report/component-15.html).

[^remaining-life]: Conditional remaining life expectancy is the number of additional years a person who has already reached a given age can expect to live, using the mortality rates in a life table. In Japan in 2021, this was 19.85 years for a man aged 65 and 24.73 years for a woman aged 65. See [Table 1 of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's 2021 Abridged Life Tables](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/toukei/saikin/hw/life/life21/dl/life21-02.pdf). This is different from subtracting 65 from life expectancy at birth, and it is not a prediction of the ages at which the people in the model will die.

[^health-insurance]: Kyokai Kenpo is the employee health insurance scheme administered by the Japan Health Insurance Association. Its health insurance contribution rates for fiscal 2021 were 9.84% in Tokyo and 9.91% in Aichi; see the [2021 prefectural contribution rate table](https://www.kyoukaikenpo.or.jp/about/business/insurance_rate/rate_prefectures/r03/). Insured people aged 40–64 were also subject to a long-term care insurance contribution rate of 1.80%. Enrollment and contribution rules differ after retirement, so working-age health insurance rates cannot simply be extended to all ages.

[^medical-benefits]: In the [minutes of the 179th Medical Insurance Committee meeting on June 21, 2024](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/newpage_42146.html), the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare states that lifetime medical expenditure estimated using fiscal 2021 data was ¥26.98 million, of which approximately ¥23 million was covered by health insurance benefits. Pages 50–53 of the [accompanying PDF materials](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/content/12401000/001266889.pdf) provide medical expenditure by age, the estimation method, and remaining medical expenditure. These are undiscounted estimates from birth, constructed using that year's age-specific medical expenditure and mortality rates. The ¥19.24 million present value of household health insurance benefits in this article is an output of the Japanese model; it cannot be obtained simply by multiplying ¥23 million by the number of household members.

[^income-tax]: Resident taxes are local taxes levied by prefectures and municipalities. Their personal income component is calculated separately from national income tax; they are not property taxes paid by homeowners. The employment income deduction, social insurance deductions, and basic deduction reduce taxable income, while tax credits reduce the tax already calculated. Eligibility for a mortgage tax credit depends on the occupancy, property, and loan conditions applicable in the relevant year. This article uses the 2021 tax parameters in the Japanese calculation; the income tax and resident tax amounts are model results.

[^consumption-tax]: Japan's consumption tax is an indirect tax on transactions in goods and services. From October 2019, the standard rate was 10%, with a reduced rate of 8% for eligible food, subscription newspapers, and certain other purchases. Alcohol and dining out are excluded from the food category eligible for the reduced rate. See the [National Tax Agency's explanation of the reduced consumption tax rate](https://www.nta.go.jp/taxes/shiraberu/taxanswer/shohi/6102.htm). This article uses the rates applicable in 2021. If expenditure already includes tax, the tax component should be extracted using 10/110 or 8/108, rather than multiplying the tax-inclusive price by 10% or 8%.

[^other-insurance]: Assigning a net amount of zero to other social insurance is a neutral treatment in the central estimate, not a finding that actual contributions or benefits are zero. This calculation does not separately set out contributions and expected benefits attributable to the household for unemployment, workers' compensation, long-term care, and similar insurance. It therefore does not estimate additional net extraction from those components.

[^home-prices]: Prices come from [Tokyo Kantei's annual report on resale apartment prices for 2021](https://www.kantei.ne.jp/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/c2021.pdf), published on January 24, 2022. The figures are ¥63.33 million for Tokyo's 23 wards and ¥24.96 million for Nagoya City. Both are annual average prices standardized to 70 square meters, not uniform transaction prices for all housing types and locations. The price-to-income ratio uses modeled household cash earnings, not labor value including employer contributions.

[^property-tax]: Fixed asset tax is based on the assessed value of land, buildings, and other taxable assets, with a standard rate of 1.4%. City planning tax applies to land and buildings in the relevant areas, at a maximum rate of 0.3%. For small residential plots of up to 200 square meters per dwelling, the taxable bases can be reduced to one-sixth and one-third of assessed value, respectively. This relief does not directly apply to the full value of the building. See the [Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism's explanation of taxes on land ownership](https://www.mlit.go.jp/totikensangyo/totikensangyo_tk5_000073.html). The effective holding tax parameter of 0.30% of market value per year is a model estimate; it cannot be derived mechanically from the two statutory rates.

[^mortgage]: Loan-to-value (LTV) is the amount borrowed as a percentage of the home's price; this article uses 70%. A loan with equal installments of principal and interest has a fixed scheduled payment, while the principal and interest portions change over time. Page 4 of the Japan Housing Finance Agency's [April 2024 survey report](https://www.jhf.go.jp/files/a/public/jhf/400370422.pdf) includes historical reference average borrowing rates of 1.0% and 1.2% for the April and October 2021 surveys, respectively. These averages are estimated using the midpoints of interest rate bands and related assumptions. The 1.1% annual borrowing rate used here is not an inflation-adjusted real interest rate, nor is it a uniform quoted rate for a mortgage fixed for 30 years. The model applies this parameter to a 30-year amortization schedule and discounts the interest payments using the common timeline.

[^education]: The education component in this series uses a signed difference: the local share of household consumption spent on private education, minus 1.5%. Negative amounts are permitted when spending falls below the baseline. The difference in shares must be converted into spending during the relevant education years, then discounted and divided by lifetime labor value. A difference in consumption shares cannot be treated directly as percentage points of LCER. Japan's zero in this estimate follows from the assumption that its spending matches the baseline; it is not an independently observed result for education spending by particular families raising children in the two cities.

[^coverage]: No additional amounts for public monopolies, resource misallocation, or similar extended components could be reliably attributed to the standard household in this calculation, so core and extended LCER have the same value. Some excise taxes, housing transaction taxes, and housing regulatory premiums have not been fully included. Public services that are difficult to value in monetary terms for individual households, such as public safety and infrastructure, have also not been comprehensively valued and deducted. LCER is therefore not a complete social cost-benefit analysis. All amounts and percentages are approximate, and the totals follow the aggregate figures in the Japanese calculation.
